# The Library: what the cards compress

Knowledge cards are deliberately short; each one compresses a book or a body of practice into an essence, a skip-when, and a trap. This file points at the originals for when the compression is not enough. Every one-liner below is this repository's own words, never the author's; read the source for the author's.

Rules for this list:

- A book earns a row when a knowledge card or a path step draws on it, with two named exceptions in the last table. A library that catalogs everything teaches nothing, same rule as the [knowledge index](../knowledge/README.md).
- Read the card first, the book second. The card tells you the trap in one minute; the book earns its hours only after the card has made you curious or skeptical.
- Nothing here is reproduced. If you want the author's argument, buy the argument.

### Annotations

Each entry says what the card leaves out, the one signal that means you should go to the source, and where practitioners most often misapply the book. Read the annotation before you buy anything; two thirds of the time it will tell you the card was enough.

**Cagan, Inspired and Empowered.** The card carries the four risks and the empowerment test. What it cannot carry is the organizational argument, which is most of the second book: empowerment is a property of how a company assigns work, not of how a PM behaves, so a PM practicing empowered behavior inside a feature-factory reporting line will produce friction and no change. Go to the source when your own diagnosis keeps landing on your manager's manager. Misapplied as a licence to refuse roadmaps; the actual claim is about who owns the problem, not about whether dates exist.

**Torres, Continuous Discovery Habits.** The card carries the weekly habit and the opportunity solution tree. The book carries the interview mechanics and the assumption tests that make the tree honest, which is exactly the part teams skip when they adopt the diagram. Go to the source when your tree has stopped changing shape, because a tree that only grows is a record of your beliefs rather than of your learning. Misapplied as a mandate for a fixed number of interviews a week; cadence is the point, the count is a proxy.

**Christensen, Hall, Dillon, Duncan, Competing Against Luck.** The card carries the circumstance-motivation-outcome shape and the competitor reframe. The book carries the case histories, and the cases are the reason to read it, because the method is easy to state and hard to feel until you have watched it dissolve someone else's obvious market definition. Go to the source when your job statements keep coming out generic. Misapplied by turning every job into an emotional one; the functional job usually decides the purchase, with the emotional job deciding the loyalty.

**Singer, Shape Up.** The card carries the appetite, the shaping, and the circuit breaker. The book carries the pitch format and the scope-hammering practice, plus the staffing assumptions the whole system rests on, which is where most adoptions quietly fail. It is free online, so the cost of reading is time only. Go to the source before proposing the method to a team, because the six-week bet without the cooldown and without shaped work is just a longer sprint. Misapplied by teams that keep a backlog and a bet cycle at the same time.

**Bryar, Carr, Working Backwards.** The card carries the press release and the hard FAQ. The book carries the surrounding mechanisms, single-threaded ownership, the narrative meeting, the way documents are read in silence before discussion, and those mechanisms are what make the artifact work; the document alone becomes marketing copy written early. Go to the source when your written proposals are being skimmed rather than read. Misapplied as a template; it is a meeting protocol that happens to produce a template.

**Grove, High Output Management.** The card carries managerial leverage and output as the team's output. The book carries the operational spine, indicators paired so one catches the other's distortion, the production analogy, task-relevant maturity, and it is the most direct treatment of how a manager decides where to spend an hour. Go to the source when your calendar is full and your leverage is not. Misapplied by reading only the OKR chapter, which is the least original part of a book whose real subject is manufacturing discipline applied to knowledge work.

**Doerr, Measure What Matters.** The card carries the objective and key result structure and the scoring discipline. The book carries the adoption stories, and they are its weakness as well as its use: the cases are selected on success, so read it for the mechanics of grading and committed versus aspirational goals rather than for evidence that OKRs cause outcomes. Go to the source when your organization is adopting OKRs and you need the vocabulary everyone else will be using. Misapplied as a performance-review input, which is the one use Grove's original practice warned against.

**Moore, Crossing the Chasm.** The card carries the beachhead and the buyer-motivation split. The book carries the whole-product concept and the pragmatist-buyer psychology, which is what makes the beachhead choosable rather than merely nameable. Go to the source when your product sells fine to enthusiasts and stalls at the second reference call. Misapplied to consumer products with no reference dynamics, and misapplied again to companies whose actual problem is that the early market never formed.

**Ellis, Brown, Hacking Growth.** The card carries the one value metric and the input metrics beneath it. The book carries the experiment cadence and the cross-functional team structure, and it is at its best on the machinery of running many small tests and at its weakest on when not to. Go to the source when your team can name a growth bet but cannot run one a week. Misapplied by treating loops as universal; a loop needs a mechanism traceable actor by actor, and most products have channels rather than loops.

### Annotations

**Fitzpatrick, The Mom Test.** Short enough to finish in an evening and the highest ratio of practice change to pages in this list; the worked bad-conversation transcripts are the value. Read it before your first real interview rather than after, and run [the interview guide](../frameworks/discovery/mom-test-interview-guide.md) alongside. Misapplied as a ban on ever mentioning your product, which makes later sales conversations awkward and is not the claim.

**Rumelt, Good Strategy Bad Strategy.** Read the bad-strategy half first, because recognizing fluff, unaddressed problems, and goals mistaken for strategy is the transferable skill; the kernel is the constructive half and is already on [the worksheet](../frameworks/strategy/strategy-kernel.md). Go to the source when your leadership deck is full of ambitions nobody can act on. Misapplied by treating the kernel as a document format rather than a test you apply to whatever format you already have.

**Lafley, Martin, Playing to Win.** The cascade of five choices, from an operator's seat at a consumer-goods company, which is worth knowing because the examples are physical products with retail distribution and the translation to software is yours to do. Go to the source when where-to-play is the choice your team keeps refusing to make. Misapplied by filling all five boxes in one workshop; the choices constrain each other, so a coherent cascade takes revisions.

**Helmer, 7 Powers.** Dense, economic, and unusually honest about what does not count as advantage, which is most of what gets called a moat. Go to the source when someone asks why a competitor cannot copy this in a quarter and your answer is brand or data without a mechanism. Pair with [the audit worksheet](../frameworks/strategy/seven-powers-audit.md). Misapplied by claiming scale economies at a size where they do not yet exist.

**Ries, The Lean Startup.** Historically important and now partly absorbed into general practice; the minimum viable product idea has been damaged by a decade of misuse. Go to the source for the build-measure-learn discipline and the innovation-accounting chapters, which are the parts that aged well. Misapplied as permission to ship something bad, when the argument was about learning per unit of effort.

**Patton, User Story Mapping.** The best answer in this list to the flat-backlog problem, and the mapping technique survives whatever process wrapper your team uses. Go to the source when release scoping keeps producing a set of features that do not add up to something a user can complete. Pair with [the story map worksheet](../frameworks/prioritization/user-story-map.md). Misapplied as a one-time workshop artifact rather than a living map.

**Knapp, Zeratsky, Kowitz, Sprint.** A protocol, not a theory, and it is best read the week before you run one; [the runbook](../frameworks/discovery/design-sprint-runbook.md) covers the mechanics without the book. Go to the source when you need to persuade a group of executives to give up five consecutive days. Misapplied to questions that discovery could have answered in a morning, which is the expensive mistake this book invites.

## Standing exceptions

Two books earn a row without a card or index entry behind them, because path steps send learners to them for craft the cards do not cover.

| Book | Author | This repo's one-line take | Used by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Escaping the Build Trap | Melissa Perri | Output-measured organizations ship more and matter less; the fix is structural, not motivational | The transitioning path, for the org-context step |
| The Effective Executive | Peter Drucker | Effectiveness is a practice of choices about time, contribution, and strengths, not a temperament | The senior path, alongside Grove |

**Perri, Escaping the Build Trap.** The reason it earns an exception is that the transitioning path's first step asks what the seat owns, and the honest answer depends on whether the organization measures output or outcomes; this is the book that describes that difference structurally, incentives and roles included, rather than as a matter of PM willpower. Read it while you are interviewing for the seat, not after you have taken it, because the diagnosis is most useful as a question to ask a hiring manager.

**Drucker, The Effective Executive.** Old, short, and about knowledge work generally rather than product specifically, which is why it sits beside Grove on the senior path: Grove tells you where leverage is in an organization, Drucker tells you how a person decides what to do with their own attention when everything on the list is defensible. Read it when your problem has stopped being skill and started being allocation. The examples are dated; the argument about contribution is not.

## Podcasts

Podcasts are commute learning, not evidence. When an episode changes your mind about something in your practice product, write the change into your `learn/products/<product>/PROGRESS.md` ledger; nothing from a podcast enters a real workspace without its own source.

| Show | Host | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Lenny's Podcast | Lenny Rachitsky | Named operators describing how they actually ran the methods the cards compress, failure modes included |
| Product Thinking | Melissa Perri | The organizational layer: product operating models, leadership, why good process dies in bad structure |
| Acquired | Ben Gilbert, David Rosenthal | Long-form company histories; the strategy entries (7 Powers especially) shown at work over decades |

One discipline makes these useful rather than pleasant: an operator describing what worked at their company is a class-five belief about your company, because you cannot see the distribution, the timing, or the twenty firms that did the same thing and disappeared. Treat an episode as a hypothesis generator, and if it changes a decision, the decision still needs its own evidence in [the ladder](../skills/conductor/questions/README.md).

## How to use this file from a path

A path step names its card; the card is mandatory, the book is not. Come here when a Done-when line keeps failing and the card's compression is the reason, or when the tutor's critique cites a card you found thin. Log what you read in your PROGRESS.md ledger with one line on what changed in your next attempt, because reading that changes nothing is decoration.

**The decision rule for buying a book.** Three signals, and one is not enough. First, the same card has failed you twice on different products, which suggests the gap is in your understanding rather than in one situation. Second, the annotation above names your symptom. Third, you can say which artifact you would rewrite afterwards. If you cannot name the artifact, you want the feeling of preparing rather than the preparation, and the cheaper cure is to run a worksheet from [the frameworks layer](../frameworks/README.md) on a real decision this week.

**What to read when the answer is not a book.** A method you cannot run is a worksheet problem, not a reading problem: the frameworks layer has the run-it form of most of what is listed here, with worked examples. A method you can run but keep misjudging is a card problem, so re-read the card's trap section. A method you run well that keeps producing decisions your organization ignores is neither, and the two exception books above are where that question is actually treated.
