# Path: Design fundamentals for PMs

Audience: you have already run a product through DEFINE and into DESIGN, on this path or in real work, and you want the experience-design vocabulary that sits underneath a screen: heuristics, signifiers, the evidence class behind a cited "law," the legal floor for accessibility, content and forms, choice symmetry, and the review discipline that turns all of it into one signed decision. This path assumes you can already write an acceptance criterion and a design brief; it does not re-teach DEFINE.

Fictional product: **Kerbline**, a resident portal for Elmsworth Borough Council (invented council, invented borough) covering bin collection schedules, a missed collection report, and a paid annual garden waste subscription. Everything about it is invented, including its residents, its council, and its support desk; that is the point. Prefix invented evidence with "invented:" so the habit of labeling survives into real work.

Time: one step is an evening, the same discipline as [Foundations](path-foundations.md). Step 4 runs long on purpose: budget two evenings for it, because a genuine keyboard and screen reader walk cannot be rushed into one sitting, and rushing it is the exact failure this path exists to stop.

Before step 1: create `learn/products/kerbline/`, copy the ledger below into `PROGRESS.md` there, and read the workspace rules in [products/README.md](products/README.md). Check boxes in your copy, never in this file.

## The standing brief

Every step draws on one invented situation, so your artifacts accumulate instead of restarting each evening. Copy this block into your PROGRESS.md and treat it as the only fact base you start with; anything else a step needs, you invent and label.

Invented: Kerbline has been live for nine months and carries 38,000 registered residents out of roughly 61,000 addresses in Elmsworth. The support desk logs about 500 calls a month; a third of them are residents who could not find the "report a missed collection" form, and another fifth are residents trying to cancel the garden waste subscription, which can only be cancelled by phoning a number printed in the subscription's terms page, not from the account screen that sells it. No accessibility audit has ever been run against Kerbline. The council's own digital team has one designer and two engineers, and the stated plan for next quarter is "a redesign of the homepage," proposed with no cited evidence for why the homepage is where residents are actually getting stuck.

Those are your baselines wherever a step asks for one, labeled invented every time. As in [Foundations](path-foundations.md), the label matters more than the number: an unlabeled practice figure that gets quoted back at you as a fact has taught you the one habit this layer exists to prevent.

## Step 1: heuristics as inspection, not evidence

**Read:** [Usability Heuristics](../knowledge/design/usability-heuristics.md). Notice the trap: a heuristic pass is trained judgment, not user evidence, and the studies behind it say exactly how much that substitution costs.
**Study:** [Heuristic Evaluation](../frameworks/design/heuristic-evaluation.md), the worksheet that scores a pass against Nielsen's and Shneiderman's lists and carries the cognitive-walkthrough variant.
**Do:** run the worksheet, alone, as one evaluator, against Kerbline's "report a missed collection" flow: the entry point, the form, and the confirmation screen. Fill section 1 (scope), section 3 (findings grid) with at least four findings tagged to a named heuristic, and write the coverage caveat from section 6 in your own words in PROGRESS.md, stating the exact percentage range it names.
**Done when:** every finding names a specific heuristic by its Nielsen or Shneiderman name, not a feeling, and your coverage caveat states both the single-evaluator range and the inter-evaluator agreement range from the knowledge card's evidence section, not a rounded guess.

**Why this comes first.** Every later step in this path either runs another inspection method or feeds one, and every one of them inherits the same ceiling: one evaluator, however careful, catches a minority of what is actually wrong, and two evaluators looking at the same screen often disagree about most of what they separately find. Learning to write "one evaluator, unvalidated" honestly, before you have anything invested in the finding being complete, is the discipline the rest of this path assumes you already have.

**What good looks like.** Weak: "the missed-collection form feels confusing." That names no heuristic and gives nobody a way to check it. Strong: "invented: the form's submit button is styled identically to the disabled 'request collected again' button above it, which fails Consistency and standards, because the same visual treatment now means two different states (severity 3, evidence: screenshot MC-04)." The second version names the heuristic, the mechanism, and a way to verify it.

**Pass criteria.** A 2: four or more findings, each naming a real heuristic from the knowledge card, each with a severity on the 1 to 4 scale and a way to check it, plus a coverage caveat stated with both numbers from the evidence section. A 1: findings that are real observations but tagged to the nearest-sounding heuristic name rather than the one that actually fits, or a caveat that names only one of the two ranges. A 0: findings written as taste ("this looks dated") with no heuristic named at all.

**The trap, and its tell.** Treating your own findings list as proof the flow works once you stop finding things. The tell is a session that ends with "no more issues found" standing in for "the flow is fine": the evidence in the knowledge card says a lone evaluator running out of findings is evidence the evaluator is out of findings, not that the screen is clean. Write the coverage caveat into PROGRESS.md even when your list feels thorough, because that is exactly the moment it is most likely to be wrong.

**Time.** Ninety minutes to two hours. A panel of one is still worth running carefully; it is also worth knowing, in writing, exactly how incomplete it is.

## Step 2: signifiers into acceptance criteria

**Read:** [Interaction Design Principles](../knowledge/design/interaction-design-principles.md). The trap here, "affordance" flattened into "looks clickable," is the one the rewrite discipline in this step exists to stop.
**Study:** [Acceptance criteria](../templates/definition/acceptance-criteria.md), in particular its rule that a criterion must be executable by a tester who has read nothing else.
**Do:** take three vague criteria you invent for Kerbline's garden waste subscription screen, each written the way a rushed spec actually writes them ("the signup is easy to find," "the confirmation is clear," "cancelling feels safe"), and rewrite each one using the card's rewrite discipline: name the role, name the signifier or feedback event as a concrete, perceivable thing, name its accessible label where one exists, and put a number on the timing. Fill two full AC-n blocks from the template for your two strongest rewrites, including the edge and negative case each one implies.
**Done when:** none of your three rewritten criteria contains an adjective a reviewer could argue with ("easy," "clear," "safe"), and each one names which gulf, execution or evaluation, it closes.

**Why now.** A heuristic pass in step 1 tells you where a screen breaks a general principle; it does not give you language a tester can run against. This step is where that language gets built, and every later step in this path, the content audit, the critique, the design review record, depends on criteria written this way rather than on an adjective nobody can test.

**What good looks like.** Weak: "the subscription confirmation is clear." Strong: "invented: once payment succeeds, the resident with a confirmed address sees a banner labeled 'Garden waste collection starts [date]' within 2 seconds (ILLUSTRATIVE) of the payment screen closing, and the banner persists on the account page until the first collection date passes." The weak version describes a gulf being closed; the strong version describes what closes it, and only the second can fail a test.

**Pass criteria.** A 2: three rewrites, each naming a concrete signifier or feedback event, an accessible label where one applies, and a number; two of them carried into full given/when/then blocks with a measurable threshold and at least one edge case. A 1: rewrites that name a control but still lean on an adjective to describe its effect ("a clear confirmation banner appears"). A 0: a rewrite that restates the original vague criterion with different words and no observable event named at all.

**The trap, and its tell.** Calling a control "a good signifier" in review and treating that sentence as if it settled something. The tell is a rewrite that still contains a word like "noticeable," "obvious," or "intuitive": each one is a claim that a gulf is narrow, restated, not a description of what narrows it. If you can delete the adjective and the sentence loses its only content, you have not finished the rewrite.

**Time.** Sixty to ninety minutes. The two full AC blocks take longer than the three one-line rewrites; that ratio is correct, because the given/when/then form is where a criterion either survives contact with a tester or does not.

## Step 3: the UX-laws claim check

**Read:** [UX Laws Evidence Ledger](../knowledge/design/ux-laws-evidence.md). The trap is a poster citation standing in for a source; the ledger exists so you do not have to re-derive a paper's limits section from scratch every time someone cites its name.
**Study:** [Non-functional requirements](../templates/definition/nfr.md), section 1, where a performance budget gets a number and an owner rather than a bare "best practice" claim.
**Do:** write four sentences a rushed Kerbline spec might actually contain, each citing a named "law" to justify a decision about the collection-calendar screen or the missed-collection form ("per Fitts's law, make the report button bigger," "per Hick's law, cut the calendar down to three view options," "per the aesthetic-usability effect, the bright new calendar design means we can skip a usability round," "users don't scroll, so put everything above the fold"). For each, name its row in the ledger, its evidence class, the strongest counter-evidence the ledger cites, and the "PM action instead" line rewritten for Kerbline specifically. Land the one sentence that survives as a real NFR row in section 1 or section 5, with a number and an evidence class attached.
**Done when:** every one of your four sentences carries an evidence class from the ledger's five-term vocabulary (empirical law, perceptual regularity, judgement effect, heuristic, or adage), and at least one of your four names a claim the ledger explicitly says not to cite at all.

**Why now.** A design brief or an NFR that cites a law sounds exactly as authoritative whether the writer read the paper's limits section or copied a line off a poster; nothing in the sentence's own grammar tells a reviewer which happened. This step is where you practice telling the two apart before you are the one writing the spec, because the cost of getting it wrong lands on whoever argues your decision later, not on you the day you write it.

**What good looks like.** Weak: "per Fitts's law, make the report button bigger," left as a bare citation. Strong: "invented: Fitts's law is an empirical law for pointing and selection tasks, built and validated on exactly that, per MacKenzie's 1992 review; the report button is a single tap target, so the citation applies, but the law says nothing about whether the calendar's month-picker, a drag gesture, should also be sized by the same formula, and that control needs a different argument." The second version uses the citation's actual scope instead of its name.

**Pass criteria.** A 2: four claims, each tagged with the correct evidence class and the ledger's own counter-evidence, with the "users don't scroll" or "three-click rule" claim (if chosen) correctly identified as a claim that does not get a row at all. A 1: correct evidence classes but no counter-evidence named, so the claim is tagged but not actually checked. A 0: a citation accepted at face value because the law's name sounded authoritative.

**The trap, and its tell.** Treating "adage" as a weaker version of "empirical law" rather than a different kind of claim entirely. The tell is asking what would have to be true for the citation to be wrong: a real empirical claim answers with a number or a condition (Fitts's law breaks down at very short distances); an adage answers with a shrug (Jakob's law is just what Jakob Nielsen has observed). If your rewritten sentence cannot answer that question, you have not finished checking it.

**Time.** Sixty minutes. Short, because the work is classification, not invention; the discipline is resisting the pull to skip straight to "sounds right."

## Step 4: accessibility, the legal floor and the walk

**Read:** [Accessibility Regulation](../knowledge/design/accessibility-regulation.md). Kerbline is a public sector body's resident portal, which puts it squarely inside this card's United Kingdom, public sector row; read that row twice, and read the card's own caution that two of its ten rows are verified against primary text and the rest are not.
**Study:** [Accessibility checklist](../templates/architecture/accessibility-checklist.md). This repository has no separate accessibility audit worksheet; the checklist itself is the walk, which is why this step runs the checklist directly rather than a framework file first.
**Do:** fill section 1, scope and tools, for the missed-collection report flow: name the conformance target (use the knowledge card's practical rule, WCAG 2.2 AA, and say in one sentence why that target clears the UK public sector row even though that row's legal reference is still marked OPEN and its monitoring guidance names WCAG 2.2), name the assistive technology you are invoking for the walk, and name a walker who is not the form's own author. Then walk section 3, forms and inputs, against the actual form, checking each row with a keyboard alone and an invented screen reader pass, and fill every evidence cell with something a skeptic could open, never "looks fine."
**Done when:** section 1 names a conformance target with its source, a named assistive-technology pairing, and a walker distinct from the author; section 3 has a result, pass, fail, or "N/A because," on every row, with evidence for every pass.

**Why now.** Steps 1 through 3 all ran or cited inspection methods that are optional in the sense that a team can choose to skip them and ship anyway. This step is not optional in that sense: Kerbline is a public sector body's website, which the knowledge card's obligation map names directly, and a conformance target chosen here is either grounded in that row or it is a guess wearing a percentage sign, exactly the distinction the card's essence section opens with.

**Pass criteria.** A 2: a conformance target named with its source row from the regulation card, a walker who is not the author, and every row in section 3 carrying a real result with evidence, including at least one fail with a reason that is not "ran out of time." A 1: the checklist filled with results but no named walker distinct from the author, or a conformance target chosen without naming which row of the obligation map it rests on. A 0: every row marked pass with no evidence cells filled, which the checklist's own exit gate calls a claim, not a check.

**The trap, and its tell.** Reading the regulation card's table as a finished legal answer rather than a dated snapshot with named decay modes. The tell is citing a compliance date or a technical standard from the card without also naming its "Verified" column value and its row-specific review trigger; the card states plainly that a standard's existence is not the same as a standard's legal force, and a walk that skips straight to "we meet WCAG 2.2, so we are compliant" has made exactly the mistake the card's own trap section names.

**Time.** This is the step to budget two evenings for, as the top of this path says. One evening for section 1 and a first pass at section 3 with a keyboard; a second evening to actually run an invented screen reader pass and fill the evidence cells honestly, because a walk rushed into one sitting produces a checklist that looks complete and is not.

## Step 5: content, forms and choice symmetry

**Read:** [Content Design](../knowledge/design/content-design-and-forms.md). Content design starts from a user need checked, not assumed; a product that skips straight to wording is copyediting a decision nobody checked.
**Study:** [Content and microcopy audit](../frameworks/design/content-microcopy-audit.md) and [Choice symmetry audit](../frameworks/design/choice-symmetry-audit.md), two worksheets, run in sequence.
**Do:** run the content and microcopy audit on the missed-collection form's error, empty, and confirmation states, at least six invented strings across the three states, scored against every rule in the worksheet's step 2, including the banned-word house list. Then run the choice symmetry audit on the garden waste subscription: the accept-direction action is subscribing in-app; the decline-direction action is the invented phone-only cancellation path from the standing brief. Fill the effort grid for steps, screens, time, channel, default state, visual prominence, and wording neutrality, and tag every flag the grid produces.
**Done when:** the content audit has a pass rate computed per rule, not one blended score, with a fail list naming an owner for every failing string; the choice symmetry grid flags the channel dimension at minimum, given the invented flow, and every flag is tagged with a jurisdiction or carries a one-line reason why none applies.

**Why now.** Step 2 built the habit of naming a signifier instead of an adjective; this step applies the same discipline to words and to consent, where the cost of an untested default or an asymmetric cancel path is not a confused tap, it is a resident who cannot stop being charged, or a blame word that makes a bad day worse. Both worksheets turn a feeling ("the copy reads fine," "cancelling seems okay") into a rule a reviewer can check without having been in the room.

**What good looks like.** Weak content: "Sorry, invalid postcode. Please try again." Strong: "invented: Enter a postcode in Elmsworth Borough. We could not find [postcode entered] in our records." The first fails three rules at once, "sorry," "please," and "invalid," with no stated fix; the second states what happened and how to fix it. Weak choice symmetry: reporting the garden waste flow as "basically fine, most people don't complain." Strong: the effort grid showing a channel flag (in-app accept, phone-only decline) on its own, per the worksheet's rule that a channel switch flags regardless of any step count, which is exactly Kerbline's invented situation.

**Pass criteria.** A 2: a content audit with a computed pass rate per rule and a fail list with owners, plus a choice symmetry grid with every applicable dimension filled, a flagged channel dimension tagged to a named jurisdiction line (or a stated reason none applies), and the routing line to the compliance impact assessment named even though no such document exists yet for Kerbline. A 1: both worksheets run but the choice symmetry grid computed as a single blended verdict ("mostly fine") rather than per-dimension flags. A 0: a choice symmetry pass that flags the visual prominence of a button and stops there, missing the channel switch the standing brief hands you directly.

**The trap, and its tell.** Fixing the easiest flagged dimension and reporting the pair fixed. The tell, straight from the worksheet's own trap section: a team that evens out button weight while the phone-only cancellation channel is untouched has closed one of seven rows and called the grid clear. Re-run every dimension's ratio after any single fix, not just the one that changed.

**Time.** Two hours across both worksheets: roughly forty-five minutes for the content audit's six strings, and the rest for the choice symmetry grid, because naming the jurisdiction and routing lines honestly takes longer than filling the effort numbers.

## Step 6: critique against review

**Read:** [PM and design collaboration](../knowledge/design/pm-design-collaboration.md), the method card behind both the critique and the record this step fills.
**Study:** [Design Critique](../frameworks/design/design-critique.md), then [Design Review Record](../templates/architecture/design-review-record.md), in that order: a critique gathers feedback against agreed objectives, a review evaluates a design and can end in a go decision, and the two are never the same meeting.
**Do:** run the critique worksheet on the missed-collection flow, using invented objectives pasted from an invented design brief section 4 ("a resident can report a missed collection in under two minutes, from the home screen, without a login"). Fill the scope, the presenter's questions, the phase bar, and the outcome log, with at least four comments, at least three tied to a pasted objective. Then fill the design review record sections 1 through 6, carrying forward your step 1 heuristic findings, your step 5 content and choice symmetry flags, and your step 4 accessibility evidence into section 2 and section 3, and write one Gate 3 decision in section 6. Finish by filling section 7, the live-build review, with an invented release candidate, an invented walker who is not the implementer, and every box checked or explicitly marked out of scope.
**Done when:** the critique's outcome log shows an objective-tied share you can compute and state as a number; section 2 of the design review record links every evidence type to a worksheet you actually ran in steps 1, 4, or 5, or marks it "not run because"; section 6 names one decision with dissent recorded or explicitly noted as none; and section 7 carries a walker distinct from the implementer.

**Why now.** Every worksheet in this path produces a findings list or a flag table; none of them produces a decision. This step is where you practice the one move this entire layer has been building toward: carrying separate pieces of evidence, heuristic, content, accessibility, choice symmetry, into a single record and writing a decision that someone who was not in the room can check against what is actually on the page, rather than against how confident the room felt.

**What good looks like.** Weak: a design review record whose section 6 reads "Approved, looks good." Strong: "invented: APPROVE WITH CONDITIONS, because the missed-collection form clears its accessibility walk and its content audit, but the subscription cancellation channel flag from the choice symmetry audit is unresolved; condition: the phone-only cancel path is replaced with an in-app equivalent before Gate 4, owner [name], due [date]." The second version is checkable; the first is a mood.

**Pass criteria.** A 2: a critique outcome log with a computed objective-tied share, and a design review record whose section 2 links to real artifacts from earlier steps (not "not run" on everything), whose section 3 findings carry an evidence class of inspection or user with no severity 3 or 4 inspection finding marked closed, and whose section 6 names one decision with conditions owned and dated. A 1: the record filled but every evidence-type row in section 2 reads "not run because," which technically satisfies the template's own rule but means steps 1, 4, and 5 were not actually carried forward. A 0: section 6 left blank, or a decision with no reasoning sentence attached.

**The trap, and its tell.** Sitting in your own critique at pixel altitude and then letting the room's warm agreement stand in for the decision in section 6. The tell is an outcome log full of accepted comments and a design review record that copies "the critique went well" into section 6 instead of a reasoned APPROVE, APPROVE WITH CONDITIONS, or RETURN; a critique produces feedback, never a go, and conflating the two is the exact failure the design critique worksheet names as its own trap.

**Time.** Two to three hours: the critique session itself is the shorter half; carrying its outcome log and four prior steps' worth of evidence into the design review record's six sections, honestly, is the part that teaches the discipline.

## Capstone: a scored design review record

**Run:** [the design-review skill](../skills/design-review/SKILL.md), in Gate 3 review mode and then live-build review mode, against your completed Kerbline design review record from step 6. The skill runs with no model required, as a checklist a person walks by hand; without an AI runtime, walk its seven workflow steps yourself, in order, exactly as written.

**Done when:** every line in the design review record's own exit gate, both the Gate 3 block and the Gate 4 block, has a pass or fail written against it, each with one sentence of reasoning and the knowledge or framework card that reasoning rests on. A fail anywhere means a targeted redo of the step that should have produced the missing evidence, not an edit to the record alone; a fail that traces to no earlier step at all means this capstone session itself found a gap none of the six steps was built to catch, and that gap gets its own line in PROGRESS.md.

**Where each exit-gate line comes from.** Section 1's pasted objectives trace to step 6's critique scope. Section 2's evidence links trace to step 1 (heuristic evaluation), step 4 (accessibility checklist), and step 5 (content and choice symmetry audits); a blank link here means one of those steps was not actually carried into the record. Section 3's no-severity-3-or-4-inspection-finding-closed rule traces to step 1's coverage caveat. Section 5's handoff checklist traces to step 2's signifier and feedback-event discipline: a state or edge case named there as a concrete, perceivable thing is exactly what section 5 asks an engineer to be able to read off the spec without guessing. Section 6's decision and dissent trace to step 6's critique-versus-review distinction. Section 8's routing traces to step 5's own routing rule for choice symmetry flags. The Gate 4 block traces to step 4's accessibility walk and step 6's live-build section directly; a box checked there with no corresponding walk in either step is the one failure mode this capstone is most likely to surface on a first pass.

**Pass criteria for the session itself.** A 2 across every exit-gate line, each with its one-sentence reasoning and its cited card, landed in PROGRESS.md with the weakest line named even when every line passed. A session with no fail anywhere is not automatically a triumph: check whether the record's evidence links in section 2 point at worksheets you actually ran with real effort, or at worksheets marked "not run because" across the board, which would pass the template's own letter while skipping the evidence this whole path was built to produce.

**The trap, and its tell.** Scoring your own record generously because you wrote every line of it yourself. The tell is a session where every exit-gate line gets a pass with a one-clause reason and no card cited: a real pass names the specific evidence on the page, heuristic finding F1, content audit string C3, the channel flag from the choice symmetry grid, not a general sense that the record looks thorough. If you cannot point at the exact cell a line's pass rests on, treat the line as a fail and go find the evidence, or go produce it.

**Time.** One session of ninety minutes to walk every line, plus whatever redos it names. As in Foundations, budget a second evening for this one too; a capstone that passes clean on a first pass through this path usually means the design review record was handed a thin set of worksheets and graded against them generously rather than against what they were supposed to contain.

## When you are ready to leave this path

Not when the boxes are ticked. Three signals. First, you can look at a spec line that cites a law, a component, or a usability claim, and say in one sentence what evidence class it actually rests on and what a reviewer should ask for instead. Second, you have caught yourself writing "looks fine" or "feels safe" about a real screen, at work or elsewhere, and rewritten it into something a tester or a regulator could actually check before anyone asked you to. Third, at least one exit-gate line in the capstone failed on your first pass and you traced it back to the step that should have produced the missing evidence, rather than patching the record alone, because a path completed without a single traced failure taught you the vocabulary and not the habit of carrying evidence forward.

This path runs alongside [Foundations](path-foundations.md), [Transitioning](path-transitioning.md), and [Senior sharpening](path-senior.md) rather than after them: the vocabulary here applies to whichever stage-sequence path you are already running, the moment that path's product has a screen worth looking at.
