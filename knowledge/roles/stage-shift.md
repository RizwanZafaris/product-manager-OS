# Stage Shift: The Same Title, Three Different Jobs

A Product Manager at a twelve-person startup, a two-hundred-person scale-up, and a twelve-thousand-person enterprise share a business card and almost nothing else. Stage changes the scope, the evidence bar, the document weight, and the definition of success faster than any promotion does. Read this card before hiring across stages, before taking a job across stages, and before judging a colleague who came from a different one.

The underlying distinction is Steve Blank's: a startup is an organization searching for a working business model, while an established company executes one it has already found. Search and execution reward opposite habits, and the awkward middle, the scale-up, rewards a third: turning what worked once into what works repeatably.

## The three stages

**Startup (search).** One product, few customers, no margin for process. The PM, where one exists at all, does discovery, definition, support, and half of marketing in the same week; Cagan's observation is that the real product manager is usually a founder, whatever the org chart says. Documents stay light by necessity: the [one-pager](../../templates/definition/one-pager.md) is the ceiling, and the [decision log](../../templates/execution/decision-log.md) earns its keep because memory is the only institution. The gates in [os/STAGE-GATES.md](../../os/STAGE-GATES.md) still apply; they just pass in hours, not weeks.

**Scale-up (repeatability).** The model works and the company is hiring faster than it can transmit culture. The PM's job becomes converting founder intuition into written strategy, installing just enough process to stop repeating mistakes, and handing off pieces of a job they used to do whole. Molly Graham's scaling advice describes the emotional core: growth means repeatedly giving away work you love to people who will do it differently. This is the stage where document weight rises legitimately, and where the [WHICH-DOCUMENT](../../os/WHICH-DOCUMENT.md) weight tree stops being optional.

**Enterprise (execution and renewal).** Portfolio, install base, regulators, and a thousand people who can veto but not approve. The PM's craft shifts to stakeholder navigation, sequencing against dependencies, and protecting discovery time from a calendar that wants to eat it. Document weight is at maximum for good reasons (blast radius, compliance, audit) and bad ones (fear laundering itself as rigor). The enterprise PM who cannot tell those apart drowns; the [assumptions register](../../templates/definition/assumptions-register.md) and gate discipline exist to keep honest weight and cut theater.

## What shifts, by dimension

| Dimension | Startup | Scale-up | Enterprise |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scope per PM | The whole product, plus stray hats | A product area, newly fenced | A slice of a workflow, precisely fenced |
| Evidence bar | Founder conviction plus a few live customers | Cohorts and repeatable signals | Statistical, plus regulatory and audit trail |
| Document weight | Logged decisions and one-pagers | One-pagers and PRDs | Full stacks, per the weight tree |
| Decision distance | The decider is at the next desk | The decider is two rooms away | The decider is a committee calendar |
| Process | Overhead until proven otherwise | Installed just-in-time, always late | Pre-installed; the job is pruning it |
| Career risk | The company dies | Your role is reorganized twice a year | You become excellent at an internal game |

## What the same title means at each stage

| Title | Startup | Scale-up | Enterprise |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product Manager | Runs the product, reports to a founder | Runs an area end to end | Runs a feature slice inside a line |
| Director of Product | Often the first PM, zero reports | Player-coach over the first real teams | Runs a line and a hiring bar, per [the ladder](ladder.md) |
| VP of Product | Ships personally, title is mostly external | Builds the org while running it | Runs strategy and leaders, ships nothing directly |

## The trap: title inflation

Titles inflate downhill: the smaller the company, the grander the title for the same scope. A Head of Product over one product and no reports, a VP whose organization is three people, a CPO at a seed round: none of these is dishonest, but all of them translate badly. The trap catches both directions. Companies hiring from startups read big titles at face value and level the hire wrong; operators moving from enterprise to startup discover their old title's scope does not exist there at any title.

The defense is mechanical: level by scope, never by title. Four questions recover the truth: what did you own, what did you decide without asking, which documents did you produce and who consumed them, and who did you manage. Map the answers onto [ladder.md](ladder.md) and the stage tables above, and note that moving to a bigger company at a smaller title is frequently a scope increase wearing a modesty costume. Anyone writing a [first 90 days plan](../../templates/planning/first-90-days.md) across a stage boundary should plan for the stage, not the title: the mandate line in that template is where the real level shows up.

## Sources

- Steve Blank, The Four Steps to the Epiphany (2005) and later writing: the search-versus-execution definition of a startup that this card's stages hang on.
- Marty Cagan, Inspired (2017): the startup, growth-stage, and enterprise treatment of product work, and the founder-as-real-PM observation. See [empowered product teams](../cagan-product-teams.md).
- Molly Graham's essays on scaling teams: the give-the-work-away framing of the scale-up stage, paraphrased above.
- Geoffrey Moore, via [Crossing the Chasm](../crossing-the-chasm.md): market stage and company stage are different clocks; a scale-up selling to mainstream buyers runs both shifts at once.
