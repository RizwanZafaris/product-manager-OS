# Jobs to Be Done

Based on the ideas of Clayton Christensen (The Innovator's Solution, 2003), Tony Ulwick (What Customers Want, 2005), and Bob Moesta (Demand-Side Sales 101, 2020).

## The essence

People do not buy products because of who they are. They hire products to make progress in a specific circumstance, and they fire them when something else makes that progress better. The unit of analysis is the job, not the customer segment and not the product category. This reframes competition: a spreadsheet competes with a notebook, a phone call, and doing nothing, because all four are candidates for the same job. Market share measured against the category can look healthy while the job quietly walks out the door.

The tradition has three strands worth telling apart. Christensen supplied the framing: understand the circumstance and the progress sought, and product decisions stop being guesses about demographics. Ulwick made it measurable: break a job into desired outcomes, then score each outcome by how important it is and how poorly it is currently served; the gap is the opportunity. Moesta made it interviewable: reconstruct the timeline of a real past purchase and surface the four forces acting on the switch, the push of the current struggle, the pull of the new solution, the anxiety about the new, and the habit of the present. Progress happens when push and pull outweigh anxiety and habit.

A job well stated is stable over decades even as solutions churn, which is what makes it a foundation worth writing down.

The mechanism is a change of causal claim. Demographic segmentation says that people like this buy things like this, which is a correlation and cannot tell you what to build; a correlation has no lever in it. Circumstance says that when this situation arises, a person needs this progress, which names a cause and therefore names a trigger, a moment, and a competitor. The practical payoff arrives immediately in three places: the problem statement stops containing your product, the competitive set expands to include everything the customer currently does instead, and the launch plan gains a moment to aim at rather than an audience to broadcast to.

That expansion of the competitive set is the frame's sharpest practical edge, and it is uncomfortable on purpose. Most competitive analysis compares you to companies that also sell software, because those are the ones with websites to screenshot. The job asks what the customer did last time, and the honest answer is usually free, ugly, and already installed.

The word progress is doing precise work and is not a synonym for benefit. Progress is directional and situated: from a state the person finds intolerable, toward one they can picture. It carries emotional and social dimensions alongside the functional one, which is why the same functional job can be served by two products with wildly different adoption. Being seen by your team as the person who has the numbers ready is part of the job of preparing the review, and a product that serves the functional half while embarrassing the performer loses to a spreadsheet every time.

## Where it came from

Two lineages that are usually told as one. Tony Ulwick's came first and came from consulting: a search for a way to specify what customers want that engineers could design against, which produced a fixed grammar for outcome statements and a survey to rank them. Clayton Christensen's came from academic research into why competent companies lose to worse products, and it needed a unit of analysis that was not the product category. The two met in the late 1990s and the popular story has flattened them into a single school.

The flattening matters when you try to run the method. Ask a practitioner what jobs to be done is and you will get either a survey instrument or an interview technique depending on which lineage they learned, and the two disagree about sample sizes, about whether jobs decompose into outcomes, and about whether the output is a ranked list or a story. Pick the strand that fits your question rather than trying to hold both, and say which one you picked.

## What the frame assumes

1. **There was a moment of switching to reconstruct.** The method's evidence comes from a real decision with a before and an after. For a habit that accreted gradually, or a tool imposed by an employer, there is no switch and the interview produces a rationalization instead of a timeline.
2. **Memory of a real purchase beats prediction of a future one.** This is the strongest and best-supported assumption in the tradition, and it is why the interview asks what happened rather than what would appeal.
3. **The job is stable while solutions churn.** True at the right altitude and false at the wrong one, and the method contains no test that fixes the altitude for you. See the lying section.
4. **The performer and the buyer are the same person.** Often true for consumers and rarely true in business, where the person with the struggle, the person who signs, and the person who administers the tool have three different jobs that can conflict. A single job statement in a business context is usually three, and the missing two are where deals die.

## When to use it

- When framing a problem: state the job and the circumstance before naming any solution, so the problem statement survives the first design review.
- When building personas: anchor the archetype in the progress sought, not in demographics that correlate with nothing.
- When planning interviews: Moesta-style switch interviews about a real past decision produce evidence; hypothetical questions produce politeness.

**Skip it when:** the work is maintenance, a mandated change, or a fix to something already broken. The job is settled and interviewing to rediscover it burns sessions you will want later. Reach for it when the question is what to build, not when the question is how fast the known thing can be repaired.

## A worked case, ILLUSTRATIVE

Tandem is an invented tool that helps freelance designers send contracts and get paid. All numbers are made up. The company had been losing deals and had a competitive deck naming two rival products; the win-loss file showed twenty-three losses in two quarters, of which six went to those two rivals and fifteen went to what the sales notes recorded as no decision.

Switch interviews with nine recent buyers changed the shape of the problem. The circumstance that produced a purchase was not a designer shopping for contract software; it was a designer who had just been paid late or not at all, usually after an informal agreement, usually within about three weeks of the incident. The job was to get paid for work already agreed without becoming the kind of person who chases friends for money. The competitor in fourteen of those fifteen no-decision losses was a document template plus a payment link, which is free, familiar, and quietly excellent at the functional half of the job.

That reframing moved the roadmap and the messaging in the same direction. Under the category frame, the product needed feature parity with two rivals: clause libraries, e-signature options, template variety. Under the job frame, parity was irrelevant to fifteen of the twenty-three losses, and the levers were anxiety and habit rather than pull. Anxiety was concentrated on one thing, that sending a formal contract would make a friendly client feel accused; habit was the existing template file the designer had already customized. The two highest-value pieces of work became a tone-controlled contract that reads like a confirmation rather than a legal notice, and a first-run flow that imports the template the designer already uses.

The competitive consequence is worth stating separately, because it is the part that survives the specific example. Once the free workaround is on the board, the question changes from how to beat two rivals to how to beat good enough, and good enough is a much harder opponent: it has no sales team, no price, and years of the customer's own configuration invested in it. Products lose to it silently, because a loss to a workaround leaves no competitor name in the sales notes and gets filed as no decision.

The teaching point is the arithmetic of the forces, not the features. Push was strong, at three, because a specific unpaid invoice had triggered the search. Pull was moderate, at two. Anxiety was three and habit was two, so the stay side outweighed the switch side, and every euro spent on making the promise more attractive was spent on the side that was already winning. When the stay side is heavier, better marketing makes the funnel wider and the conversion rate worse, which is exactly what the company had observed and had been treating as a messaging problem.

## The trap: job stories without interview evidence

The format is seductive: "when [circumstance], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]." It takes four minutes to write in a conference room and reads exactly like the ones written from real interviews. That is the problem. A job story is a compression of evidence, and a compression of nothing is still nothing wearing good syntax. Teams fill a wall with invented jobs, feel the warmth of customer-centricity, and proceed to build for a customer who does not exist. The rule this repository enforces through its templates: every job statement carries a count of the interviews behind it, and a job with zero is labeled an assumption, which is a fine thing to be as long as it says so.

The tell is uniformity. Real interview evidence produces job statements of uneven length and awkward specificity, because a real circumstance has a Tuesday in it and a person who was already late. Invented ones are tidy, parallel, and phrased at the same altitude, because one person wrote them all in an afternoon with a template on the screen. A wall of eight beautifully parallel job stories is nearly always a wall of hypotheses, and calling them that costs nothing and prevents a quarter of confident building.

## Other ways it fails, and the tell for each

- **The product inside the job.** "Get my expenses submitted in the app" is a task in your interface, not a job. The tell: the statement stops making sense if your company shuts down. A real job survives your death.
- **Altitude drift upward.** "Grow my business" is true of everyone and rules nothing out, so it can never be contradicted by evidence and never narrows a decision. The tell: two competing roadmap options both serve the job equally well.
- **Altitude drift downward.** "Export a CSV of last month's invoices" is a step, so it locks the current solution's shape into the supposedly solution-free statement. The tell: the job names a format, a file, or a screen.
- **The persona with a new hat.** Demographics get rewritten in job syntax and nothing changes, because "as a small business owner I want to save time" is a demographic with a verb. The tell: swap the person and the sentence still holds.
- **Team-authored anxieties.** Participants rarely volunteer what scares them, so the anxiety column gets filled in from the team's imagination and every entry is flattering. The tell: all anxieties are about price or setup effort, and none is about status, blame, or looking foolish.
- **The hypothetical interview.** The session drifts into what would you want, and the transcript fills with feature requests. The tell: no dates, no names of other people, no mention of what happened next. A real switch story is full of both.
- **The job that never changes anything.** The statement is agreed, admired, pinned to the wall, and the roadmap it was meant to reorder proceeds untouched. The tell: no document downstream cites it, and nobody can name a decision that went differently because of it.
- **Evidence that stops at the buyers you already have.** The sample is drawn from the customer list, so the findings describe people the product already suits. The tell: no interviews with people who chose the workaround and stayed.
- **One job for a multi-party purchase.** The tell: your evidence comes entirely from users, your losses come entirely from procurement, and nobody has written down the job of the person who signs.

## How it lies

The method's honest weakness is altitude. There is no procedure inside jobs to be done that determines the right level of abstraction for a job statement, so the analyst chooses it, and the choice determines the answer. Stated broadly enough, a job explains every observation and predicts nothing; stated narrowly enough, it smuggles the current solution into the supposedly neutral description. Practitioners resolve this with judgment and craft, which is fine as long as nobody claims the output is a discovered fact. The workable discipline is to write the job at two altitudes and ask which one would have ruled out something the team actually wanted to do. A job statement that has never eliminated an option is decoration.

It also lies through the interview's own machinery. A switch interview reconstructs a decision, and reconstructions are edited: people supply a coherent narrative with clear causes because that is how memory works, not because the decision was tidy. The forces are real signals and their strengths are estimates from a story told after the fact. Treat the strengths as ordinal rather than metric, and be suspicious of any switch story where nothing was confused.

The last distortion is selection. Interviewing switchers finds out why people who switched, switched. The people who never considered you, and the people who tried and quietly left, hold most of the information about the ceiling of the market, and they are the hardest to recruit. A jobs practice built exclusively on recent buyers will keep discovering that the product's current strengths are what customers want.

One habit converts the frame from vocabulary into practice: write the competitive set as a list of what customers did last time, with counts, and put it beside the vendor list in the same document. The gap between the two lists is usually where the roadmap is wrong.

## What good looks like

Mapping the steps, scoring struggle and weighing the four forces are the [job map worksheet](../frameworks/discovery/jtbd-job-map.md)'s procedure. What follows is how you tell a job that was found from a job that was composed.

| Done well | The version that looks the same and is not |
|---|---|
| A job statement a competitor's customers would recognize as theirs too | A job statement only your users would recognize, which is a usage description in job clothing |
| A circumstance with a trigger, a moment, and a person who was already struggling | A circumstance that reads as a market segment with a verb attached |
| Interviews that disagree somewhere, with the disagreement left on the page | Interviews that agree completely, which usually means the questions carried the answers |
| An anxiety somebody has priced: what removing it would cost, and who pays | An anxiety answered by a sentence in the launch copy |
| A competitive set that includes the spreadsheet, the phone call, and doing nothing | A competitive set that includes the three vendors in the analyst quadrant |
| In business contexts, separate jobs for the performer, the buyer, and the administrator | One job statement standing in for three people with conflicting incentives |
| A job that has ruled out something the team wanted to build | A job that every option on the roadmap serves equally well |

The last row is the cheapest test in the table and the one most often skipped. A job statement that fails to exclude anything has cost the team a fortnight and bought it a vocabulary, because the whole value of the frame is that it makes some plausible work indefensible. Run the test directly: read the roadmap against the statement and count the items it argues against. Zero means the statement was written wide enough to keep every stakeholder, which is a political achievement and a research failure. The anxiety row fails in a related way. Anxiety is the force that most often decides adoption and the only one a team can address without building anything, so leaving it unpriced is choosing to spend engineering months on pull when a review step and a visible undo would have moved more people.

## Where it sits in the loop

- Stage: DISCOVER, before anything is framed. The job is the first artifact, because every later document inherits its altitude.
- Upstream: recruiting from real recent switchers, planned in the [user research plan](../templates/discovery/user-research-plan.md) and run with the [interview guide](../templates/discovery/interview-guide.md).
- Downstream: [problem framing](../templates/discovery/problem-framing.md) takes the job statement, [personas](../templates/discovery/personas.md) take the circumstance, [competitive analysis](../templates/discovery/competitive-analysis.md) takes the redefined competitive set, and the [win-loss review](../templates/operate/win-loss-review.md) tests all three against money.
- On trial at [Gate 1: problem worth solving](../os/STAGE-GATES.md), which asks for evidence counts precisely because a job story costs four minutes to invent.
- Run as a session by the [user interview skill](../skills/user-interview/SKILL.md), which keeps the conversation on what happened rather than what would appeal.

## What it is not for

- **Sizing a market.** A job is not a number of people. Sizing needs a defined population and a rate, from market sizing work, and a job statement will not supply either.
- **Pricing.** The job explains why someone hires a solution, not what they will pay for it; willingness to pay is a separate study with separate instruments.
- **Choosing between two features that serve the same job.** Once the job is settled the question becomes prioritization, and this frame has no scoring in it. Hand it to opportunity scoring or a scoring sheet.
- **Retention diagnosis on its own.** The forces model the moment of switching in, and churn is a switch out with its own push, pull, anxiety, and habit. Run the interview again from the other direction rather than assuming the first result inverts.
- **Mandated or maintenance work.** The job is settled and the question is delivery. Nothing here helps, and the interview slots are worth more elsewhere.

## Variants worth knowing

- **Outcome-driven innovation**, Tony Ulwick's formalization: decompose the job into desired outcome statements with a fixed grammar, survey importance and satisfaction, and rank by the gap. The most quantitative strand, and the one that connects directly to opportunity scoring.
- **Switch interviews and the timeline**, from Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek: reconstruct the four forces from first thought to purchase. The most useful strand for a small team, because it needs no survey infrastructure.
- **Job stories**, popularized by Alan Klement and by Paul Adams at Intercom as an alternative to user stories: circumstance, motivation, expected outcome, with no persona. Powerful and the easiest of all these to fake, which is what the trap section is about.
- **Small hire and big hire**, a distinction from the Moesta tradition: the big hire is the purchase, the small hire is the moment of actually using it for the job. Products die in the gap between them, and only the second one is progress.
- **Circumstance-based segmentation**, Christensen's own emphasis: segment the market by situation rather than by customer attributes. Worth knowing because it is the strand that changes how a company reports on itself, not just how a team runs discovery.
