# Crossing the Chasm

Based on the ideas in Crossing the Chasm by Geoffrey Moore (1991, revised 2014).

## The essence

Moore's observation is that a technology product does not diffuse smoothly across one market. It moves through segments that buy for different reasons, and the dangerous gap sits between the early adopters and the early majority. Early adopters buy change itself: they want the advantage of being first and will tolerate rough edges to get it. The early majority buys improvement to how they already work, wants references from people like themselves, and hears "visionary" as a warning label. The chasm is that the references early adopters generate do not transfer: mainstream buyers discount them precisely because they came from enthusiasts.

The prescribed crossing is deliberately narrow. Pick one beachhead segment, small enough to dominate and specific enough that its members talk to each other, and become the obvious default there before generalizing. Winning a niche produces the self-referencing word of mouth that mainstream buyers require; spreading effort across every interested segment produces a thin presence everywhere and references nowhere. The whole-product idea completes the move: the mainstream buyer is purchasing the complete solution to their problem, integrations, support, and migration included, not the core technology that impressed the visionary.

The frame is a segmentation claim, not a growth schedule. Its value in a launch plan is that it forces a named first cohort and a named reason that cohort buys, instead of a broadcast to everyone the product could conceivably help.

## When to use it

- When filling the beachhead section of a GTM plan, to force the first cohort down to a segment specific enough that its members cite each other as references.
- When early traction stalls after an enthusiastic start, as a diagnosis: strong pilot logos with no mainstream follow-on is the chasm's signature, not a sales-execution problem.
- When positioning against a named alternative, because the mainstream buyer frames every purchase as a replacement for something they already use, and positioning that names no incumbent is positioning for early adopters only.

**Skip it when:** the product is not a discontinuous innovation. A faster or cheaper version of something the buyer already understands has no chasm to cross; the mainstream can evaluate it on its own terms from day one, and beachhead discipline just delays revenue that was already reachable.

## The trap: the beachhead that is secretly everyone

The most common failure is nominal compliance: a "beachhead" defined as "mid-market companies that need better analytics," which is a market, not a niche, and cannot generate self-referencing word of mouth because its members have never heard of each other. The tell is the reference test: if the second customer in the segment would not recognize the first customer's name, the segment is too wide to compound. The subtler trap is treating the model as a calendar, expecting the chasm at a fixed point in time and reorganizing for the mainstream while the actual buyers are still early adopters. The segments are defined by why people buy, not by when, and the only evidence that the crossing has begun is a mainstream buyer citing another mainstream buyer as the reason they signed.
