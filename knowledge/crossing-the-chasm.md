# Crossing the Chasm

Based on the ideas in Crossing the Chasm by Geoffrey Moore (1991, revised 2014).

## The essence

Moore's observation is that a technology product does not diffuse smoothly across one market. It moves through segments that buy for different reasons, and the dangerous gap sits between the early adopters and the early majority. Early adopters buy change itself: they want the advantage of being first and will tolerate rough edges to get it. The early majority buys improvement to how they already work, wants references from people like themselves, and hears "visionary" as a warning label. The chasm is that the references early adopters generate do not transfer: mainstream buyers discount them precisely because they came from enthusiasts.

The prescribed crossing is deliberately narrow. Pick one beachhead segment, small enough to dominate and specific enough that its members talk to each other, and become the obvious default there before generalizing. Winning a niche produces the self-referencing word of mouth that mainstream buyers require; spreading effort across every interested segment produces a thin presence everywhere and references nowhere. The whole-product idea completes the move: the mainstream buyer is purchasing the complete solution to their problem, integrations, support, and migration included, not the core technology that impressed the visionary.

The frame is a segmentation claim, not a growth schedule. Its value in a launch plan is that it forces a named first cohort and a named reason that cohort buys, instead of a broadcast to everyone the product could conceivably help.

Underneath the segments is a claim about risk, and it is the part worth carrying. An early adopter is buying an advantage and accepts risk as the price of getting it early; a pragmatist is buying the removal of risk, and to them an unproven product is not a cheaper option but a worse one at any price. That inversion explains everything else in the model. It explains why the same feature list persuades one group and alarms the other, why the pragmatist wants a reference from someone whose job resembles theirs rather than from an impressive logo, and why discounting fails to bridge the gap: a discount reduces cost, and cost was never the objection.

The whole-product idea follows from the same claim. What removes risk for a pragmatist is not the core capability but everything around it: the migration path, the integration with the system they already run, a support arrangement, a consultant who has done this before, a name in their industry who did it last year. A company that has built only the core has built the part its early adopters valued and none of the part the mainstream is buying.

## Where it came from

Moore was building on Everett Rogers's diffusion of innovations, published in 1962, which described adoption as a smooth curve running from innovators through to laggards. Rogers's curve is continuous by construction, and Moore's contribution, drawn from consulting work with high-technology companies in Silicon Valley in the 1980s, was to argue that between two of those groups the curve breaks, because the two groups do not accept each other's evidence.

That context matters for deciding whether the model applies to you. It comes from enterprise technology sales with long consideration cycles, human sales channels, and industry peer groups that actually convene. Where those conditions hold, the mechanism is strong. Where a product spreads through a marketplace listing, a network effect, or a free tier a user can try in four minutes without asking anyone, the reference mechanism is much weaker and the model's prescriptions are correspondingly less binding.

## What the model assumes

1. **The innovation is discontinuous.** It asks the buyer to change how they work. A better version of something familiar has no chasm, because the mainstream can evaluate it against the thing it replaces from day one, which is exactly what the skip line says.
2. **Members of the beachhead segment can hear about each other.** The whole crossing runs on self-referencing word of mouth, so a segment whose members never convene, compare notes, or share a trade publication cannot compound, however precisely you have defined it on a slide.
3. **References dominate the purchase.** True in high-consideration business buying with a committee and a procurement step. Weak where the individual user adopts alone and the company finds out later.
4. **The company can decline revenue.** Beachhead discipline is a series of refusals, and every refusal is a real deal with a real number attached. A company that cannot say no to out-of-segment revenue has not adopted the strategy, whatever the plan says.

## When to use it

- When filling the beachhead section of a GTM plan, to force the first cohort down to a segment specific enough that its members cite each other as references.
- When early traction stalls after an enthusiastic start, as a diagnosis: strong pilot logos with no mainstream follow-on is the chasm's signature, not a sales-execution problem.
- When positioning against a named alternative, because the mainstream buyer frames every purchase as a replacement for something they already use, and positioning that names no incumbent is positioning for early adopters only.

**Skip it when:** the product is not a discontinuous innovation. A faster or cheaper version of something the buyer already understands has no chasm to cross; the mainstream can evaluate it on its own terms from day one, and beachhead discipline just delays revenue that was already reachable.

## A worked case, ILLUSTRATIVE

Barrowfield is an invented product that predicts equipment failures in warehouses from vibration sensors, and every number below is made up. Its first eighteen months looked like success: twelve paid pilots across nine industries, enthusiastic sponsors, conference invitations, and two renewals out of the twelve. The board read the renewal rate as a product-quality problem and funded a reliability push.

The pattern says something else. Twelve sponsors across nine industries is twelve people who each wanted to be first in their own company, which is the definition of an early-adopter portfolio. Nothing about that population supports a reference: a sponsor in food logistics does not persuade a plant manager in pharmaceuticals, and neither of them convinces the pragmatist two floors down who has been running a maintenance schedule for eleven years.

The beachhead the team eventually chose was narrow enough to be uncomfortable: cold-chain food distribution warehouses in one region, running two shifts, where a compressor failure spoils inventory within hours. Uncomfortable is the right word, because it excluded eight of the nine industries already paying them. The reference test was the deciding argument, and it is mechanical: name your next two target customers and ask whether the second would recognize the first. In this segment they would; they meet at the same association, they use the same two refrigeration contractors, and they lose money in the same specific way.

Two consequences followed that the core technology had nothing to do with. The whole product for this segment required an integration with the maintenance scheduling system all of them ran, a written procedure their contractors could follow when an alert fired, and a report their food safety auditor would accept. None of that had mattered to a single early-adopter pilot, and all of it stood between the product and a pragmatist's signature.

The refusal was the expensive part, and it is where most adoptions of this strategy actually fail. Declining the eight other industries meant declining renewals the company could have had, in a year when it needed revenue, on the argument that the wrong revenue costs roadmap. That is a real trade and not a slogan, which is why the decision belongs in a decision log with the options that lost, so that the third time a large out-of-segment deal appears, the reasoning is available rather than re-argued from scratch.

A year later, of eight new customers, six named a prior customer as the reason they took the meeting. That is the only number in this case that indicates a crossing has begun. Revenue growth does not distinguish a crossing from a run of unrelated enthusiast deals, which is precisely the mistake the first eighteen months encoded.

## The trap: the beachhead that is secretly everyone

The most common failure is nominal compliance: a "beachhead" defined as "mid-market companies that need better analytics," which is a market, not a niche, and cannot generate self-referencing word of mouth because its members have never heard of each other. The tell is the reference test: if the second customer in the segment would not recognize the first customer's name, the segment is too wide to compound. The subtler trap is treating the model as a calendar, expecting the chasm at a fixed point in time and reorganizing for the mainstream while the actual buyers are still early adopters. The segments are defined by why people buy, not by when, and the only evidence that the crossing has begun is a mainstream buyer citing another mainstream buyer as the reason they signed.

The reason nominal beachheads survive review is that they are written to be approvable rather than to be true. A narrow segment looks like a small number in a plan, and nobody is promoted for proposing a smaller market, so the definition widens by one adjective per revision until it is a market again. The counter is to make the reference test the acceptance criterion for the segment definition, because it is the one test a widened definition cannot pass.

## Other ways it fails, and the tell for each

- **The core sold without the whole product.** The team ships the capability and leaves integration, migration, and support to the customer, then reports that the mainstream is slow. The tell: every closed deal required a bespoke professional-services arrangement that nobody has productized.
- **Premature generalization.** One mainstream win is read as the crossing completed, and the next quarter's plan addresses everyone. The tell: the second segment was chosen for its size rather than for its adjacency to the first.
- **Compensation working against the strategy.** Sales are paid on any signed logo, so out-of-segment deals arrive continuously and consume the roadmap. The tell: a roadmap whose last three items each serve one customer in a different industry.
- **The logo mistaken for the reference.** A recognizable name is announced, and no buyer in the segment can be introduced to a peer who will actually take the call. The tell: case studies with a company name and no named person willing to speak.
- **Chasm language as an excuse.** Weak demand gets reframed as a stage of adoption, which converts a falsifiable problem into a narrative about timing. The tell: the word chasm appearing in a board update with no segment named beside it.
- **The segment chosen for access rather than for pain.** The team picks a niche where it happens to know people, not one where the failure is expensive. The tell: nobody in the segment can put a number on what the problem costs them per month.
- **Applying it to a self-serve product.** Beachhead discipline is imposed on a product people adopt individually in minutes, delaying reach for a reference mechanism that is not the binding constraint. The tell: the purchase involves no committee and no procurement step.
- **The beachhead that was never exited.** The niche is won, the whole product fits it perfectly, and every subsequent product decision deepens the fit until moving is impossible. The tell: two years in the segment and no experiment in an adjacent one.
- **A pilot mistaken for a purchase.** Enthusiast budget buys a trial that never becomes an operational commitment, and the pipeline reports it as revenue. The tell: deals that renew only when the original sponsor is still in the role.
- **Positioning written for the enthusiast.** The messaging promises transformation and novelty to an audience whose main question is who else like me has done this. The tell: the site leads with what is new rather than with what is replaced.

## How it lies

The model was built from enterprise technology in a specific era, and it presents its mechanism as a property of markets rather than of that channel. Consumer products, self-serve tools, and anything with a genuine network effect diffuse through mechanisms Moore's frame does not describe, and a team that applies beachhead discipline to a product whose real constraint is virality will spend a year narrowing when it should have been widening. Check the assumption list before the prescription.

It also lies through narrative selection. The famous examples are companies that crossed, and their stories are told backwards from success, which is exactly the shape of argument that cannot distinguish a strategy from a survivor. Plenty of companies picked a narrow beachhead, dominated it, and discovered the niche was the whole market. Nothing in the model prices that risk, and the honest version of beachhead selection includes a sentence about what the second and third segments are and why entering them will be easier from here.

Worth stating in the plan, then: what would have to be true for the niche to be a trap rather than a beachhead, and what early sign would show it. A segment with no adjacency argument is a business, possibly a good one, and should be chosen with open eyes rather than as the first step of a growth story nobody has checked.

The subtlest distortion is that the chasm is unfalsifiable as commonly used. Slow growth after an early rush is consistent with a chasm and equally consistent with a product nobody needs, an unaffordable price, or a broken sales motion. The model is only diagnostic when its specific signature is present: enthusiastic early buyers, weak renewals, and pragmatists who ask for peer references you cannot supply. Without that pattern, calling it the chasm is a way to keep a plan alive rather than to test it.

The strategy is also a sequencing claim about your own product, not only about buyers. What you build for the beachhead becomes most of the whole product for the next segment, or it does not, and knowing which is true before you commit is the difference between a bowling alley and a cul-de-sac.

## What good looks like

| Done well | The version that looks the same and is not |
|---|---|
| A segment whose second customer would recognize the first | A segment described by size, geography, and a need |
| Named where the segment's members convene and compare notes | An assertion that the segment is well connected |
| A whole-product list with each gap owned, including partners | A feature roadmap plus an assumption that services will cope |
| Out-of-segment deals declined, and the declines counted | Out-of-segment deals accepted as validation of breadth |
| References that are people who will take a peer's call | References that are logos on a slide |
| The next two adjacent segments named, with the reason each is easier from here | A plan to address the wider market once traction arrives |
| Crossing evidence measured as mainstream buyers citing mainstream buyers | Crossing evidence measured as revenue growth |

## What it is not for

- **Continuous innovation.** Stated in the skip line and worth repeating: no behavior change, no chasm, and the discipline costs revenue that was already available.
- **Consumer and self-serve products.** Where adoption is individual, instant, and social, the binding constraint is rarely peer reference in a professional segment.
- **Internal platforms.** The adopters are colleagues, the mandate is organizational, and the relevant model is migration and incentive design rather than market diffusion.
- **Pricing.** The frame explains what the mainstream buys and says nothing about what it will pay; pragmatists are risk-averse, which is not the same as price-sensitive.
- **Market sizing.** A beachhead is a starting position, not an estimate. Sizing the niche and sizing the market are different exercises and both are needed before the niche is chosen.

## Variants worth knowing

- **Rogers's diffusion of innovations** (1962), the parent model, with a fuller account of what makes an innovation spread quickly: relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability. Useful even where the chasm does not apply.
- **The bowling alley and the tornado**, from Moore's Inside the Tornado (1995): after the first niche, adjacent segments fall in sequence because each shares part of the whole product with the last, until the mainstream shifts at once. This is where the second and third segments get chosen deliberately rather than opportunistically.
- **Disruption theory**, Clayton Christensen's separate account of why incumbents lose. It shares vocabulary with Moore and explains a different mechanism, low-end and new-market footholds rather than reference transfer, and conflating the two produces strategy that satisfies neither.
- **Obviously Awesome positioning**, April Dunford's method: position from competitive alternatives through unique attributes to a market category. It supplies the message the pragmatist needs and pairs naturally with a beachhead choice.
- **Minimum viable segment**, the lean-startup restatement: the smallest group of customers with the same problem who talk to each other. Same reference logic, lighter apparatus, and easier to sell inside an early-stage company.
