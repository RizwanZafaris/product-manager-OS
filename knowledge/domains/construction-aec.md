# Construction and AEC

Construction and AEC software serves the people who design, estimate, build, or pay for a physical project: architects and engineers, general contractors, subcontractors, and the owner who is funding all of it. The first thing that separates this domain from most software is who the customer actually is. A project, not a company, is often the real unit of account, so licence seats, data ownership, and even which vendor gets paid can reset every time a job closes and a new one starts. The second is that the contract is the data model: an AIA payment application, a NEC4 or FIDIC clause, or a lien-waiver form is not paperwork sitting beside the product, it is the schema the product has to represent correctly or the payment does not move and the lien right does not release. The third is that almost every record this software creates, an RFI, a daily log, a photo, a change order, may end up as evidence in a dispute years after the work is done, so immutability, timestamps, and who-said-what-when are requirements, not nice-to-haves. A PM who treats this as ordinary B2B SaaS with a construction skin will build a product that logs in fine and loses every contract dispute it was supposed to help win.

## Questions a PM must ask

1. Who pays and who uses, on this specific project, and whose data is it when the project closes? The owner, the general contractor (GC), and the subcontractor can each be the buyer depending on the deal, and a licence bought by a GC for one job frequently does not travel with that GC to the next one, because the project company or the owner may hold the account.
2. Which contract form governs this workflow, and does the product encode its actual clauses? AIA Contract Documents dominate US private work (A201-2017 general conditions; the pay application itself is G702-1992 and G703-1992, or G702S-2017 and G703S-2017 for the contractor-subcontractor variant, as of 2026-09-11, confirm current editions on aiacontracts.com), ConsensusDocs is a common alternative, NEC governs much UK public-sector and infrastructure work (NEC4 remains current for new UK work, as of 2026-09-11, with no NEC5 announced) and Hong Kong public works (NEC4 has been required for works contracts above the Quotation Limit for tenders invited from mid-2022, with the Development Bureau's Hong Kong Edition of the NEC4 ECC live since July 2023 and mandatory for NEC ECC works contracts tendered from July 2024; the Bureau announced in November 2024 that full mandatory NEC adoption across all Hong Kong public-works contracts and consultancies above the Quotation Limit takes effect from Q4 2026 onward, a date this card is written just ahead of, so confirm whether that quarter has landed and many running Hong Kong contracts still operate under NEC3), and FIDIC (2017 second editions govern much international work; FIDIC issued further amendments effective 1 January 2023, which apply to the reprinted forms and do not retroactively alter a contract already signed on the original 2017 text). Each turns notices, time bars, and change orders into deadlines with legal consequences, and "generic workflow" is not a substitute for the clause that actually applies; confirm the edition named in the executed contract, as of 2026-09-11.
3. How does money actually move? Payment runs against a schedule of values inside a pay application, a share is withheld as retainage until an agreed completion point, and payment is commonly conditioned on lien waivers, a conditional waiver for the period being paid and an unconditional waiver for the period already paid. Several US states, California among them, prescribe the exact statutory wording those waivers must use (California Civil Code sections 8132 through 8138, as of 2026-09-11, confirm with counsel), so a waiver the product generates in free text can be legally worthless in that state. Joint checks and prompt-payment statutes add their own clocks. In the UK, the Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996 as amended in 2009 makes the payment notice and any pay less notice, served on time, decide the sum due; a late notice can make the applied-for amount payable (as of 2026-09-11, confirm with counsel).
4. Which records will become evidence, and does the product treat them that way? RFIs, submittals, change orders, daily logs, and site photos are exactly the artifacts a delay claim or a defect suit reconstructs a timeline from. Immutability once submitted, accurate timestamps, clear attribution, and retention through the applicable statute of repose are product requirements, not an audit afterthought.
5. What does the field actually need, and who decides whether it gets adopted? Offline capability, gloves-on usability, sunlight-readable screens, shared devices passed between crew members, and multilingual support are the baseline, and the superintendent on site, not the project executive in the office, decides in practice whether the tool gets used or gets ignored in favour of a paper clipboard.
6. Does this project carry public-work duties? Federal and DC construction contracts over $2,000, and federally assisted projects funded under a Davis-Bacon Related Act, carry prevailing-wage rules and weekly certified payroll with a signed Statement of Compliance under 29 CFR 5.5; DOL's Form WH-347 is the optional standard format (as of 2026-09-11, confirm with counsel). Coverage is decided per funding source, not by "federal money" in general. Public work also brings disadvantaged-business participation reporting and public bid procedures, which the product's payroll and compliance surfaces need to represent correctly, not approximate.
7. What safety records does the product need to hold, and for whom? OSHA injury and illness recordkeeping in the US runs under 29 CFR Part 1904, and in Great Britain the Construction (Design and Management) Regulations 2015 assign specific duties to clients, principal designers, and principal contractors, both current as of 2026-09-11; confirm with counsel the applicable regime for any other jurisdiction, since a product that touches site safety logs is touching a regulated record, not a checklist.
8. Can the model this product uses actually talk to the models everyone else on the project uses? Firms on a shared project routinely run different design and field software, so ISO 19650 information management (current edition ISO 19650-1:2018, as of 2026-09-11, confirm with counsel) and IFC data exchange (the open schema for sharing building models between tools) decide whether a change made in one system is even visible in another, and a product that only speaks its own proprietary format is quietly making itself the last one added to a project's stack, not the first.

## Gatekeepers

- **The owner's project controls team and the lender's draw inspector.** Release funding against verified progress, and can hold a draw over an incomplete lien-waiver package or a schedule slip the inspector does not believe.
- **The GC's project executive and superintendent.** The executive owns the contract and the schedule of values; the superintendent decides, day to day, whether the field actually uses the tool, and either can stop a workflow the office designed without them.
- **Sureties and insurers.** A performance or payment bond puts a surety behind the contractor's obligations, and a surety that loses confidence in a contractor's financial controls can restrict bonding capacity, which stops that contractor from bidding new work at all.
- **Authorities having jurisdiction.** Building departments issue the permits and run the inspections a project cannot proceed without, on their own schedule, not the product's.
- **Lien and prompt-payment law, state by state (or the equivalent statutory payment regime elsewhere).** Sets waiver forms, notice deadlines, and payment timing duties that differ by jurisdiction and that a generic contract-management workflow will get wrong if it assumes one state's rule is universal (as of 2026-09-11, confirm with counsel per state).
- **Prevailing-wage and public-procurement agencies.** Gate whether a contractor can bid, and continue to gate payment, on public work.

## Metrics that matter

| Metric | What it tells you | How it lies |
|---|---|---|
| Active projects and seats per project | Adoption and account health | A count inflated by many small jobs looks identical to real breadth on major work |
| Pay-application cycle time, submission to payment | Whether the payment workflow actually speeds up cash flow | A fast headline number can exclude retainage held back to project closeout, which is where the real cash is stuck |
| RFI and change-order turnaround | Whether the field is getting answers fast enough to keep working | Counting time to closure, not to resolution, rewards a reopened item that was closed prematurely and hides the delay it caused |
| Daily-log completion rate by superintendent | Field engagement with the tool | A log filled in from memory on Friday afternoon passes the completion check and is worthless as a contemporaneous record |
| Share of records with an attributed, timestamped audit trail | Dispute readiness | A high completion rate says nothing about whether the attribution and timestamp would actually survive cross-examination |
| Net revenue retention across project cycles | Whether accounts grow or shrink over time | Naturally lumpy with project starts and finishes, so a quarter read in isolation can look like churn when it is a normal gap between jobs |

## Reading

- **Bent Flyvbjerg and Dan Gardner, *How Big Things Get Done* (2023).** A data-driven look at why megaprojects overrun on cost and schedule so consistently, arguing that thorough, slow planning followed by fast execution beats the more common pattern of a rushed plan and a long, improvised build. For a PM, the useful reframe is that a construction product's real job is often reducing the number of surprises discovered mid-build, not accelerating the build itself.
- **McKinsey Global Institute, *Reinventing Construction: A Route to Higher Productivity* (February 2017).** A widely cited industry analysis of why construction productivity has lagged most other sectors for decades, pointing to fragmentation, underinvestment in digital tools, and a project-by-project structure that makes it hard for any one firm to capture the return on standardising a process. It is the clearest explanation of why this domain's tools sell against organisational inertia as much as against a competing product.
