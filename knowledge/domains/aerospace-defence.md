# Aerospace and defence

Aerospace and defence covers four adjacent worlds under one card: software built for militaries and the primes that build for them, avionics and aircraft software, airline flight operations and maintenance, and space systems, folded into question 8 and the spectrum gatekeeper rather than split out on its own. Three questions decide almost everything else, and none is an engineering question. Who may see the code is a legal question, settled by export-control law before a line reaches a foreign teammate, a subcontractor, or a demo audience. Where the product may run is an accreditation question, settled by a security authority before the first deployment to a live network. And a change to certified software is a certification event: the fix a team calls a one-line patch can trigger a re-verification cycle measured in weeks, not a deploy measured in minutes. A team that treats this domain like ordinary SaaS finds out the difference at the worst moment, after a commitment has already been made to a customer who cannot waive any of it.

## Questions a PM must ask

1. Is any part of this product export-controlled: under the US Munitions List (ITAR, 22 CFR Parts 120 to 130) or the Commerce Control List (EAR, 15 CFR Parts 730 to 774); under the UK's military and dual-use controls (the Export Control Order 2008, including the UK Military List, plus assimilated Regulation 428/2009 in Great Britain, while Regulation (EU) 2021/821 still applies in Northern Ireland under the Windsor Framework); or under EU member states' own national military-list controls (Common Position 2008/944/CFSP) and Regulation (EU) 2021/821, all as of 2026-09-11? And who on the team, which vendors, which cloud region, and which demo audience is cleared to see it? A contractor in the wrong repository can be an export violation, and liability can attach to the individual who shared it, not only the company; confirm with export-control counsel.
2. What accreditation gates deployment: a Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification (CMMC) level required by the contract's DFARS clause, a DoD cloud Impact Level or FedRAMP authorization, or an Authority to Operate signed by a named official? CMMC's rollout is itself unsettled: as of 2026-09-11 the Program rule (32 CFR Part 170) and DFARS Phase 1 self-assessment are in force, but mandatory third-party certification and the phases after it were suspended on 13 July 2026 pending a 60-day Reform Task Force review due around mid-September 2026; verify the current phase before committing a roadmap date, and confirm with counsel.
3. Where will this actually run, air-gapped, classified, or disconnected at a forward edge, and what replaces the assumptions software engineering takes for granted there: telemetry, continuous deployment, a SaaS dependency, an automatic crash report? A product built on a phone-home assumption does not degrade gracefully offline; it simply does not work, and the team usually finds out during the accreditation review rather than before it.
4. Does any part of this command or fly something that flies, an aircraft, a missile, an uncrewed system? If so, what software level does the system safety assessment (ARP4754A with ARP4761; ARP4754B supersedes it, but FAA AC 20-174 still names A as of 2026-09-11) assign, since that level sets the DO-178C objectives for airborne software that commands the flight, DO-278A for ground software such as a ground station or mission planner, or a military programme's own airworthiness criteria (for example MIL-HDBK-516D, published 26 May 2026 and superseding 516C, though a programme's certification basis may still call for the earlier revision) where neither applies? DO-254 covers the hardware and DO-330 a build tool needing qualification; agree the revision and the applicable standard with the certification authority. The same logic reaches a flying asset already in service: a maintenance record is an airworthiness record, not an internal log, and a minimum equipment list (US: 14 CFR 91.213 or 121.628, atop the records duties in Parts 43 and 91.417; EU: Part-M, Part-CAMO, Part-145) decides which open defect may legally depart with the flight, as of 2026-09-11; verify against the operator's ops specs.
5. Who buys, who uses, and who decides: a programme office, a contracting officer, and an operator or flight crew are frequently four people with four incentives, and a prototype funded under an Other Transaction Authority runs on a different clock than a programme of record funded through the annual appropriations cycle. Confusing the two is how an operator's favourite demo never reaches a production contract.
6. What does the contract say happens to the data this product produces and the IP it embeds? DFARS technical-data-rights clauses can leave the government with rights to modify, share, or recompete work a team still considers proprietary, as of 2026-09-11; verify which clause applies, and a PM who has not read the clause is negotiating against terms nobody on the team has seen; confirm with counsel.
7. If any part of the system selects and engages targets, or will be integrated into a weapon that does, what does DoD Directive 3000.09 require (reissued 25 January 2023; NSPM-11 of 5 June 2026 ordered an update within 90 days, none published as of 2026-09-11; verify and confirm with counsel)? The directive asks for senior review by USD(P), USD(R&E), and the Vice Chairman of the Joint Chiefs before formal development, and by USD(P), USD(A&S), and the Vice Chairman before fielding; it excludes cyberspace capabilities, unarmed platforms, unguided and manually guided munitions, mines, unexploded ordnance, and other non-weapon systems, so a recommend-only decision aid sits under other DoD AI policy instead, not this directive. Does an allied customer's own policy require more in the cases it does cover? "The operator can always override it" is a design intention, not the documented review the directive asks a programme to complete.
8. If any part of the product touches space, what does it owe four regimes: a spectrum filing with the ITU and the national regulator, a launch or re-entry licence (in the US, FAA 14 CFR Part 450; legacy Parts 415, 417, 431, and 435 licences ran to 9 March 2026, so Part 450 is now the only route, as of 2026-09-11), an orbital-debris disposal plan (in the US, the FCC's five-year post-mission disposal rule for LEO satellites below 2,000 km, FCC 22-74, binding on satellites launched after 29 September 2024, including non-US satellites seeking US market access, as of 2026-09-11), and, for an imaging payload, a remote-sensing licence (in the US, NOAA's commercial remote-sensing licence under 15 CFR Part 960, as of 2026-09-11)? None of these reports to the others; confirm with counsel.

## Gatekeepers

- **Export-control authorities**, the State Department's DDTC for ITAR items, the Commerce Department's BIS for EAR dual-use items, the UK's Export Control Joint Unit, or the national competent authority of the EU member state where the exporter is established, since the EU has no single licensing body. Can block a hire, a vendor, a cloud region, or a demo audience from lawfully seeing the product, and a violation can carry personal liability alongside any corporate fine.
- **Accreditors and authorising officials**, who sign a CMMC result, a DoD cloud Impact Level determination, or an Authority to Operate, plus the third-party assessors who run the review where one is required. Can stop a deployment outright regardless of how complete the software is, and a lapsed accreditation can stop a system already in production.
- **Airworthiness authorities**, the FAA, EASA, and military airworthiness authorities, plus their designees. Can refuse to certify a change to flight software regardless of lab performance, and can ground a fielded aircraft on their finding alone.
- **Contracting officers and programme offices**, who hold the authority to obligate money and accept a deliverable under the contract's own terms. Can reject a deliverable that fails a data-rights or spec clause even when it performs exactly as demonstrated.
- **Primes, acting as integrators**, the larger contractor a smaller team's product plugs into. Can decide a subsystem does not make the next integration baseline, ending the relationship regardless of its quality.
- **Spectrum and orbital regulators**, the ITU and national spectrum regulators, and in the US the FCC and FAA for launch, re-entry, and orbital-debris disposal. Can deny a filing that stops a satellite from ever operating legally, however far the engineering has progressed.

## Metrics that matter

| Metric | What it tells you | How it lies |
|---|---|---|
| Time to Authority to Operate | How long from ready to actually deployable | Counted from submission, it hides months of pre-work getting the package accreditation-ready |
| Deployment frequency to the accredited environment | Whether the team ships to where the mission runs | Counting deploys to unclassified staging inflates the number; only the accredited environment counts |
| Certification traceability coverage (requirements to test) | Whether a DO-178C (or equivalent) package is ready for review | Coverage without independent verification looks complete on a dashboard, then fails the first reviewer's actual read |
| Prototype-to-programme-of-record transition rate | Whether an Other Transaction Authority prototype survives into a funded programme | A high rate of demonstrations can sit beside near-zero conversion into appropriated funding |
| Operator task time in field exercises | Whether the tool helps the person using it, under real conditions | A friendly lab benchmark rarely survives degraded connectivity or an unfamiliar operator |
| Aircraft or mission availability and dispatch reliability | Whether the fleet is available when needed | Excludes defects deferred under a minimum equipment list, so a fleet looks healthier than its open-defect list supports |
| Accreditation currency across the supply chain | Whether subcontractors, not just the prime, can legally handle the data they touch | A prime's certification says nothing about a subcontractor two tiers down still working from an unaccredited laptop |

## Reading

- **Christian Brose, *The Kill Chain* (Hachette Books, 2020).** Argues that what decides whether a capability reaches an operator is rarely the technology and almost always the acquisition timeline built around a previous era's threat. A PM's job here is not finished when the prototype impresses in a field exercise; the slower problem is the transition Question 5 names, and a roadmap ending at the demo has not planned the path to a paying customer.
- **Leanna Rierson, *Developing Safety-Critical Software: A Practical Guide for Aviation Software and DO-178C Compliance* (CRC Press, 2013).** A working guide to what DO-178C actually asks a team to produce, not a summary of its headline requirements. The discipline it describes, evidence traced from requirement to code to test rather than intention asserted after the fact, is what an accreditor or a contracting officer's reviewer asks for even on a system that never leaves the ground.

**Conductor overlay:** DISCOVER-1 (name the person, since the operator is rarely the programme office that funded the product or the officer who pays for it), DESIGN-3 (where PII lives, sharpened here into where any data lives, since a data class here is also an export-control question), DESIGN-5 (who we wait on, since the accreditor carries its own committed timeline, not a formality at the end of the schedule), and BUILD-1 (criteria demonstrated, since in a certified system "demonstrated" means evidence a reviewer will accept, not a passing test on a laptop).

**Worked example (ILLUSTRATIVE):** the identity-and-egress slice this card's first and third questions add to that spec's section 4, none of it real:

- User identifier in events: role_session_id, a pseudonymous identifier issued by the customer's own identity system and rotated per exercise, never a name, service number, or device serial
- Environment separation: the accredited network has no staging environment; QA runs on an accredited replica maintained by the customer, never on production data
- Egress (a fifth bullet this domain adds beyond the template's four): none by default; any export runs through the customer's own periodic, human-reviewed data-release procedure, and through export-control review when technical data crosses a border, not an analytics pipeline setting

A generic instrumentation spec treats identifiers and egress as a tooling choice. This one treats them as the gate: the identifier must survive an accreditor's re-identification review, and the egress bullet says nothing leaves without the customer's security officer, and export-control counsel where technical data is involved, signing off first.
