# Edtech

Edtech's founding tension is that engagement is easy to measure and learning is not, and the two part company exactly where product decisions get made. A feature that raises minutes-per-day can lower learning-per-minute, and only one of those numbers appears on the growth dashboard by default. The domain's second structural fact mirrors healthtech: the buyer is not the user. In K-12, districts purchase on procurement cycles and evidence claims; teachers decide daily whether the tool actually gets used; students experience it. A product that wins any one of the three and loses the others churns at renewal.

## Questions a PM must ask

1. What is the efficacy claim, stated as a learning outcome against a comparison, and what tier of evidence backs it? US federal law (ESSA) defines tiers from promising rationale up to strong experimental evidence, and district buyers increasingly ask which tier you hold.
2. Who is the buyer, who is the daily decider, and who is the user, in a named district? The district signs, the teacher adopts or quietly shelves, the student learns or does not. Three constituencies, three value propositions, one product.
3. What is the procurement calendar in our target segment? District budgets, RFP windows, pilot-to-purchase timelines, and the summer implementation window set the sales rhythm; a launch that misses the buying season waits a year.
4. What student data do we touch, and under which regime: FERPA for education records, COPPA for under-13 data collection, state student-privacy laws, and the district's own data-privacy agreement? Districts sign DPAs before pilots, not after.
5. Does it work in the classroom's real conditions: shared devices, weak networks, thirty students, a teacher with four minutes of setup time? The demo environment is a lie this domain tells itself constantly.
6. What is the implementation and training load on teachers, and who carries it? Fidelity of implementation is the hidden variable behind most efficacy disputes: the product "didn't work" in classrooms where it was never really used as designed.
7. Is it accessible: WCAG conformance, Section 508 where public money is involved, and support for the students furthest from the average? In education this is both a legal gate and the point.
8. What does renewal actually depend on: usage reports, test-score movement, teacher sentiment, or a budget line surviving? Find out before building the dashboard.

## Gatekeepers

- **District procurement.** RFPs, pilot committees, DPAs, board approvals, and budget cycles; the process is the gatekeeper even when everyone in it loves you.
- **Student-privacy regimes.** FERPA, COPPA, and a thickening layer of state laws; a privacy misstep in this domain is a trust ending, not a fine.
- **Evidence standards.** ESSA evidence tiers and district research-review processes gate what you may claim and sometimes what they may buy.
- **Accessibility law.** Section 508 and WCAG expectations bind public purchasers and therefore you.
- **Teachers.** No statute, all power: the daily adoption decision is theirs, and no contract clause compels it.

## Metrics that matter

| Metric | What it tells you | How it lies |
|---|---|---|
| Learning gains vs comparison group | Whether the efficacy claim is real | Without a comparison, growth is maturation plus teaching; both would happen anyway |
| Assigned-context weekly active use | Whether teachers actually run it | Whole-school license counts hide that three classrooms carry the number |
| Implementation fidelity | Whether the product was used as designed | Rarely measured, then blamed; instrument it or every efficacy debate is unwinnable |
| District renewal rate | The metric the business survives on | Lags classroom reality by a full year; leading indicators live in usage and teacher sentiment |
| Teacher setup time and weekly overhead | The real price teachers pay | Self-reported estimates from champions understate it; watch a non-champion |
| Time-to-first-classroom-use after purchase | Whether the summer window converted | A license used from October was a September sale wasted |

## Reading

- **Failure to Disrupt**, Justin Reich (2020). The domain's necessary cold shower: two decades of technologies that were going to transform learning at scale, examined against what actually happened, and why tools amplify existing capacity rather than substituting for it. Read before writing any "revolutionize learning" sentence.
- **Disrupting Class**, Clayton Christensen, Michael Horn, and Curtis Johnson (2008). The optimistic thesis Reich is answering: disruption theory applied to schooling, arguing that software personalizes where the classroom standardizes. Hold the two books in tension; the truth of your product sits somewhere between them.
