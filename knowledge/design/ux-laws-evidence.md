# UX Laws Evidence Ledger

Based on the primary sources named in Reading below: the psychology and human-computer-interaction research on experience design that a spec's cited "law" actually traces back to, from Fitts (1954) through a 2022 meta-analysis. Explained here in this repository's own words.

## The essence

A spec that writes "per Fitts's law, make the button bigger" and a spec that writes "per Jakob's law, keep navigation where users expect it" are citing two different kinds of thing wearing the same grammar. One traces to a controlled experiment with a formula that predicts a number. The other traces to a practitioner's talk with no controlled study behind it at all. Read side by side in a design brief, both look like evidence. Only one is.

The popularization pipeline that produced the modern "UX laws" genre, most visibly the poster set at lawsofux.com, compresses a decades-long, argued-over research literature into one paragraph and one number per law, because a poster has no room for a paper's limitations section. That compression is not neutral. It strips the boundary conditions the original authors stated (Fitts's law was built and validated on pointing tasks, not on reading or decision time), drops the papers that later contested the finding (Cowan's four chunks against Miller's seven), and occasionally inherits nothing but the name from an original author who never wrote the empirical claim at all (Jakob's law, Tesler's law). None of that makes the underlying research wrong. It makes a poster citation, on its own, a bad citation.

This ledger exists so a person filling a template does not have to re-derive that history from scratch. Each row is a law a PM is likely to see cited in this repository's own documents, with what its source literature actually supports, its strongest published counter-evidence, and what to do at the keyboard instead of quoting a number.

## Where it came from

Two histories run through this table, and telling them apart is the whole point. The first is the primary literature itself: Fitts (1954) and Hick (1952) come out of mid-century experimental psychology building information-theoretic models of human performance; Miller (1956) and its rebuttal in Cowan (2001, 2010) come out of memory research; Wertheimer (1923) and its centennial review in Wagemans et al. (2012) come out of Gestalt perception; Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier (1993) and its 2022 meta-analytic update come out of judgment-and-decision research; Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd (2010) is a meta-analysis answering a specific, cited consumer-psychology claim. Each of these is a paper with a method section, a sample size, and a set of authors who can be argued with.

The second history is the HCI and UX-practice literature that adopted, renamed, and packaged some of these findings for designers: MacKenzie's 1992 review put Fitts's law into the Shannon-formulation form HCI researchers now use; Nielsen's Norman Group and, later, Jon Yablonski's Laws of UX site turned a subset of both histories into named, citable "laws" for a design audience. That second layer is where the trouble starts. It is not dishonest, but it is a summary of a summary, written for people who will not read the original paper, and every summary drops information. Two items on this ledger, Jakob's law and Tesler's law, never had a first history at all: they are practitioner adages that the second layer treats exactly like the first.

## When to use it

- When a design brief, an NFR, or a spec cites a "UX law" to justify a decision, use this ledger to check what the cited law actually supports before the citation goes in.
- When running a heuristic evaluation and a finding leans on one of these names, tag it with the evidence class from this table rather than treating every heuristic as equally proven.
- When a stakeholder pushes back on a design decision by quoting a poster, use the counter-evidence column to have the actual argument instead of a citation fight.

**Skip it when:** the decision already has direct evidence from this product, a usability test, an A/B result, a support-ticket pattern. A "law" is a prior, useful when you have nothing else; it should lose to your own data every time your own data exists.

## The ledger

Evidence class in this table uses five terms: an **empirical law** is a quantitative, replicated relationship from controlled experiments; a **perceptual regularity** is a qualitative, well-documented pattern in how perception works, not always reduced to a formula; a **judgement effect** is a measured bias in retrospective or comparative judgment, real but often smaller, narrower, or more contested than its popular telling; a **heuristic** is a design rule of thumb distilled from a research literature or from vendor material rather than from one controlled result; an **adage** is a practitioner observation with no controlled study behind it at all. Read against this repository's broader evidence-class vocabulary, the first three rest on research evidence, heuristic corresponds to practitioner heuristic (or, where noted, a vendor claim wearing that heuristic's clothing), and adage stays adage.

| Law, origin and year | What it actually claims | Evidence class | Strongest counter-evidence | Common misuse | The PM action instead |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fitts's law** (Fitts, 1954; Shannon reformulation, MacKenzie, 1992) | Time to move to and select a target is a linear function of the log2 of the ratio of distance to target width, the "index of difficulty," derived from Shannon's information theorem 17. MacKenzie's ID = log2(D/W + 1) is the form HCI research now recommends over Fitts's original. | Empirical law | MacKenzie's own review reports the model breaking down at very short distances and very small or very large targets, and needing separate treatment for two-dimensional targets, approach angle, and device: the six studies it surveys used the mouse, trackball, joystick, touchpad, helmet-mounted sight and eye tracker, not a touchscreen. It was built and validated on pointing and selection with a pointer, nothing else. A screen edge or corner acts as an effectively infinite-width target for a pointer-driven device, because the cursor's motion stops at the physical bezel, a property that does not hold for touch, where a finger can overshoot past the glass edge. | Cited to justify "make everything bigger" for interactions that are not a discrete point-and-select movement: a drag path, a keyboard shortcut, a scroll gesture. Also cited for touch targets directly, when MacKenzie's own review studied pointer devices, not touch. | Apply it only to pointer-driven pointing and selection targets, and treat any touch-target guidance as a separate, unreviewed claim until sourced. Size the smallest frequently used touch target to the platform minimum, do not assume a touch screen's edges or corners behave as effectively infinite targets the way a pointer's do, and confirm width-versus-distance trade-offs on the real device class, not from the formula alone. |
| **Hick's law** (Hick, 1952), kept separate from Hyman (1953) | Choice reaction time grows with the log2 of the number of equally likely alternatives, measured originally on a conventional choice-reaction task with up to ten alternatives. | Empirical law, narrow domain | Wanyu Liu, Julien Gori, Rioul, Beaudouin-Lafon and Guiard's CHI 2020 review argues the relationship holds narrowly and speaks against the popular "fewer choices is always faster" reading of it for most HCI tasks, because visual search and decision quality, not raw count, dominate real menus. Proctor and Schneider's 2018 review adds that stimulus-response compatibility, practice, and very large option sets change the relationship measurably. | Presented, including by poster sites, as one 1952 study with Hyman folded in as a footnote. Hick (1952) and Hyman (1953) are two separately conducted studies that later researchers combined into "the Hick-Hyman law"; the combined form was never Hick's own claim, and the "pair" framing hides that. | Use it to justify grouping and progressive disclosure in a menu with dozens of items, not to argue a five-item menu should become three. For a real decision, measure time-to-select on the actual menu rather than predicting it from option count. |
| **Miller's "seven plus or minus two"** (Miller, 1956), against Cowan (2001, 2010) | Miller's paper reports that the span of absolute judgment for unidimensional stimuli, and separately, immediate memory span, both cluster near seven items, and that recoding information into chunks lets a person hold more than seven units of meaning. | Empirical law, scope later narrowed | Cowan's 2001 review and 2010 follow-up argue that once rehearsal and chunking strategies are controlled for, central working-memory capacity is closer to three to five chunks, not seven. Miller himself, in the same 1956 paper, called the recurrence of "seven" across unrelated tasks a possible coincidence rather than a deep constant. | Quoted as "never put more than seven items in a menu" or "seven tabs, maximum." The paper concerns absolute judgment and recall span; choosing a visible menu item is a recognition task, governed by different limits than the ones Miller measured. | Do not cap a menu, nav bar, or form at seven fields on this citation alone. Group related items into small chunks, Cowan's estimate is about three to five, research evidence, then validate the real screen with usability testing rather than a fixed count. |
| **Sweller's cognitive load theory** (Sweller, 1988) | In problem solving, an approach that spends working-memory capacity searching for a solution (means-ends analysis) leaves too little capacity to build the schema that produces learning, making it a poor way to teach even when it eventually finds the answer. | Heuristic, an analogy borrowed from instructional design | The original paper compares conventional goal-specific problem solving (means-ends analysis) with goal-free problems, backed by a computational model; it is about schema acquisition in learning, and says nothing about screen density, navigation depth, or feature count, which is what interface work cites it for. | Invoked as a blanket justification for any "reduce cognitive load" recommendation, as if the 1988 paper measured interface complexity directly. | Use "cognitive load" as a working-memory framing for why a task should be broken into steps, and say plainly the transfer from instructional psychology to interface design is an analogy, not the paper's own claim. Measure task completion time and error rate on the actual screen. |
| **Gestalt grouping principles** (Wertheimer, 1923; Wagemans et al., 2012) | Perception groups elements by proximity, similarity, common fate, continuity, closure, and, in later additions, common region, connectedness, and synchrony. Wertheimer's original examples show the factors can compete, with similarity able to override proximity depending on the stimulus. | Perceptual regularity | Wagemans et al.'s centennial review notes only some grouping principles have been reduced to quantitative, testable laws; several stay qualitative, and the principles can conflict in a real layout, exactly what Wertheimer's own 1923 demonstrations were built to show. | Cited as if every principle predicts perception with equal precision, or applied one at a time without checking whether two are fighting on the same screen (grouping by position in a table that also groups by color). | Use the principles to diagnose why a layout reads wrong (two things visually grouped that are not logically related). When two principles conflict in review, treat it as a testable disagreement and check comprehension with real users, not as a rule violation. |
| **Jakob's law and Tesler's law** | Jakob's law: users spend most of their time on other products, so they prefer a new one to work the way the ones they already know work; attributed to Jakob Nielsen. Tesler's law, the "law of conservation of complexity": every application carries an irreducible amount of complexity that cannot be removed, only moved between the system and the user; attributed to Larry Tesler, who described it in an interview in Dan Saffer's *Designing for Interaction*, from his work at Apple in the 1980s, after he had left Xerox PARC for Apple in 1980. | Adage | Neither has a controlled study behind it. Both are practitioner observations passed down through talks and interviews, and "what users already know" is exactly the kind of population claim that would need evidence, not a name, to support it. | Cited with the same confidence as an empirical law in a design brief, when neither can be tested or falsified the way Fitts's or Hick's law can. | This is their only real use in this repository: record which existing conventions a design keeps and which it deliberately breaks (Jakob's law), and record where a feature's irreducible complexity has landed, on the interface or on the team building it (Tesler's law), as a documented decision, not as evidence for one. |
| **Response-time limits, 0.1 / 1 / 10 seconds** (R. B. Miller, 1968, via Nielsen, 1993, citing Card, Robertson and Mackinlay, 1991) | Three thresholds for perceived system response: roughly 0.1 seconds feels instantaneous, roughly 1 second keeps a user's flow of thought unbroken, roughly 10 seconds is the outer limit of attention before the interface needs a progress indicator. | Heuristic, an engineering threshold distilled from a literature, not one experiment's effect size | The thresholds trace to R. B. Miller's 1968 systems-engineering paper, popularized by Jakob Nielsen in 1993, whose article cites Miller 1968 alongside Card, Robertson and Mackinlay's 1991 CHI paper "The information visualizer." They should never be attributed to George Miller of the "seven plus or minus two" paper above, a mix-up this ledger exists partly to prevent. | Quoted as if a single precise experiment produced exactly these three numbers, or with R. B. Miller confused for George Miller. | Use the three numbers as response-time budgets in an NFR, cite R. B. Miller through the secondary literature named here, and add a progress indicator once a wait crosses roughly 10 seconds. Do not treat any number here as a latency SLA without your own instrumentation. |
| **Doherty threshold, about 400 ms** (Doherty and Thadhani, IBM, 1982; the "400 ms" figure has no traced primary source) | Sub-second system response raises operator productivity more than proportionally. The 1982 brief argues for sub-second response, and its own reported data compares a 3.0 second response against a 0.3 second one and records a rise from 180 to 371 completed transactions per hour. | Heuristic, vendor-sourced (this repository has not fetched the 1982 original; medium confidence) | It is IBM advocacy for faster mainframe response, not an independent controlled study. No traced primary source states 400 ms specifically: the 1982 brief's own data is a 3.0-versus-0.3-second comparison, the 1979 Doherty remark carries no number at all, and IBM's own 2018 deck illustrates "400 ms" with a line quoted from the 2014 TV drama *Halt and Catch Fire*, alongside one slide crediting "0.4ms" to "IBM Research 1984." The 400 ms figure's actual origin is unresolved. | Cited as a precise, universal 400 ms budget with the authority of a controlled psychology experiment. | Use the 1982 data's direction, sub-second response drives large productivity gains in operator-style tools, as a heuristic. Name it as vendor-sourced in a spec, do not repeat "400 ms" as if it were the 1982 data's own number, and set the actual number for any given product from that product's own usability testing. |
| **Aesthetic-usability effect** (Kurosu and Kashimura, 1995, via NN/g secondary; Tuch et al., 2012) | Kurosu and Kashimura's ATM interface study had 252 people rate 26 ATM layouts on apparent ease of use and on aesthetic appeal without using them; aesthetic appeal tracked perceived ease of use more closely than the layouts' designed-in usability did. NN/g reads this as a finding that can mask real usability problems inside a study of perceived usability. | Judgement effect | Tuch, Roth, Hornbaek, Opwis and Bargas-Avila's 2012 online-shop experiment with 80 participants found the reverse direction stronger: aesthetics did not measurably change perceived usability, but usability strongly changed perceived aesthetics after use, what is usable gets judged beautiful, more than the other way round. | Cited to excuse skipping usability testing on a visually polished design, or to justify spending on visual polish instead of fixing a known usability problem. | Treat a polished prototype's high usability ratings in early testing as suspect until a task-based test with completion and error data confirms them. Never let this effect substitute for the [accessibility checklist](../../templates/architecture/accessibility-checklist.md) or a heuristic pass. |
| **Peak-end rule** (Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier, 1993; Alaybek et al., 2022 meta-analysis) | Kahneman et al.'s cold-pressor study found people's retrospective evaluation of an experience is shaped disproportionately by its most intense moment and its ending, with duration playing only a small role; most participants chose to repeat the longer trial when it had a milder ending. Alaybek et al.'s 2022 meta-analysis of 174 effect sizes puts the peak-end effect on remembered evaluation around r = 0.58, stronger than trend, first-moment, and trough effects, and reports it as comparable to the effect of the overall average (mean) score, while duration's own effect is close to nil. | Judgement effect | The effect concerns remembered, retrospective evaluation specifically, not the experience while it is happening; Alaybek et al. report the effect as robust across the boundary conditions they tested, so this ledger does not assert a weaker effect for short or low-stakes flows without a source that says so. | Used to justify tolerating a mediocre middle of a flow as long as the ending is polished, which is a deceptive pattern, letting a known-bad process persist because the after-the-fact rating will still look good, not a design improvement. | Use it to prioritize fixing the worst moment in a journey and to invest in a strong close. Never use it to excuse leaving a known bad middle in place, and note that it does not apply when the metric that matters is task success or time on task rather than remembered satisfaction. |
| **Choice overload** (Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd, 2010, meta-analysis) | A meta-analysis of 63 conditions across 50 published and unpublished experiments, N = 5,036, found a mean effect size for "more options reduce satisfaction or motivation to choose" of essentially zero, D = 0.02, with considerable variance between individual studies and no sufficient condition identified for when overload reliably appears. | Judgement effect, near-null on average | The meta-analysis's own authors note some individual studies still found real overload under specific, unresolved conditions, so the effect is not proven false, only unreliable enough that it should not be designed around by default. | One of the most frequently cited justifications in product decks for cutting options, usually citing Iyengar and Lepper's 2000 jam-tasting study rather than this meta-analysis, which folds that exact study into a literature whose average effect it found indistinguishable from zero (named here as discussed within Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd 2010; not independently fetched or verified by this ledger). | Do not cut a feature list or a pricing-tier count on a "choice overload" citation alone. If reducing options is the hypothesis, test it directly with the product's own users; the aggregate evidence says the direction of the effect cannot be assumed. |

## Do not cite

- **The three-click rule.** No source in this ledger's evidence chain supports it, and nothing traceable to a controlled study backs the number three specifically. It stays off this table because there is nothing to put in the counter-evidence column, only an absence of a primary source.
- **"Users don't scroll."** Scroll behavior is task- and content-dependent, and treating it as a fixed rule ignores exactly the kind of context this ledger's rows insist on. Absent a controlled primary source, it does not get a row either.
- **Poster lists as a citation.** Laws of UX (lawsofux.com) is licensed CC BY-NC-ND 4.0: link it, never copy its text, its structure, or its posters. Its ND term forbids adaptation and its NC term conflicts with this repository's MIT licence, so nothing from that site, or from any similar poster product, gets paraphrased into a spec as if it were the primary literature. Cite the underlying paper from this ledger's Reading section instead, or, where the row says the underlying claim is an adage, say so.

## The trap: a poster citation standing in for a source

A spec that writes "per Fitts's law" or "per the aesthetic-usability effect" reads, to the person reviewing it, exactly like a spec that cites a controlled study with a sample size and a method, because both use the same three words: "per," a proper name, and "law." The reviewer has no way to tell, from the sentence alone, whether the writer read MacKenzie's actual limits section or copied a line off a poster. That is the trap: citation-shaped text does the social work of evidence, closing the argument in a design review, without doing evidence's actual job, which is being falsifiable and bounded. The tell is mechanical. Ask what would have to be true for the citation to be wrong, and a real empirical claim answers with a number or a condition; an adage answers with a shrug.

## How it lies

The honest distortion here is not in any one paper, it is in the pipeline that carries a paper's finding from a lab into a product deck. Each hop, primary paper to review article to practitioner blog to poster to slide, keeps the headline and drops a caveat, because a caveat costs space and the headline is what gets quoted onward. Read this ledger's own counter-evidence column and the pattern repeats: MacKenzie's paper states Fitts's law's limits inside the same document that popularized it; Cowan's rebuttal of Miller, Proctor and Schneider's qualification of Hick, and Tuch's reversal of the aesthetic-usability effect are not obscure objections, they are published, findable replies sitting one search away from the finding they qualify. The distortion is not that this evidence is hidden. It is that nobody who only reads the poster goes looking for it, because the poster gives no sign anything is missing.

The second distortion is a false equivalence of confidence. A judgement effect measured at r = 0.58 across 174 effect sizes (peak-end) and a null result at D = 0.02 across 50 studies (choice overload) get cited in the same breath and the same tone as an adage with zero controlled studies behind it (Jakob's law), because the citation format does not carry a confidence interval. This table's evidence-class column exists specifically to put that confidence back into the sentence a PM writes.

## Where it sits in the loop

- Stage: ALL STAGES, gate 1. Any stage can cite one of these rows to justify a decision; the check that actually catches a weak citation is whichever gate the citing artifact, an NFR, a design brief, a heuristic evaluation, closes, not this card's own declared gate.
