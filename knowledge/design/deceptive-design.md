# Deceptive Design

Type names after Harry Brignull's deceptive.design (definitions reworded here, in this repository's own words); prevalence numbers from Mathur et al., Dark Patterns at Scale (CSCW 2019); the normative argument from Mathur, Mayer and Kshirsagar, What Makes a Dark Pattern... Dark? (CHI 2021), a card about deceptive experience design, not the DESIGN stage or Gate 3; regulatory readings paraphrased from the EU Digital Services Act, the CCPA regulations, CPPA guidance, the FTC's 2022 staff report and its 2025 Amazon order, and the UK CMA's online choice architecture paper.

> **Not legal advice.** This card teaches a PM to name a pattern, place it against four different lenses, and know when to hand the question to counsel. It never rules on whether a specific flow is lawful. The determination is made by counsel or a regulator; [compliance impact assessment](../../templates/operate/compliance-impact-assessment.md) is where the question gets recorded and routed to them, and [reg-gap-check](../../skills/reg-gap-check/SKILL.md) maps regulatory gaps for money-movement features without ruling on any of them.

## The essence

A deceptive pattern is an interface choice that narrows a person's ability to make a free, informed decision, in the direction that benefits whoever built the interface. The single idea every serious source in this field converges on, however differently each names it, is symmetry: compare the path that goes the business's way (accept, subscribe, opt in, upgrade) against the path that goes the person's way (decline, cancel, opt out, downgrade). When the second path costs more steps, more time, more screens or more channels than the first, something other than genuine preference is doing the work.

That comparison is what turns "this feels manipulative" from a taste judgment into a measurement. A taxonomy of pattern names, on its own, only tells you what a screen looks like. A step count tells you what the screen does to the distribution of choices people end up making, which is the thing every regulator named below has decided to regulate.

The field also insists, almost without exception, on judging the interface by its effect rather than the team's stated intent. A pattern that a team built to "reduce accidental cancellation" and a pattern a team built to suppress cancellation can be the identical screen. Effect-over-intent is not a rhetorical stance here; it is written into the primary sources this card cites, and it is the reason a PM cannot defend a flow by explaining what the team meant.

## Where it came from

Two lineages, run in parallel and only later drawn together. The practitioner lineage starts with Harry Brignull, a UX researcher who in 2010 began cataloguing manipulative interface patterns he was seeing across the web at darkpatterns.org, later renamed deceptive.design. His contribution was naming: a public, growing vocabulary of pattern shapes, each linked to real enforcement cases and each defined precisely enough that two people looking at the same screen would use the same word for it. It is a field guide, built by inspection, not by measurement.

The empirical lineage arrived nearly a decade later. Mathur et al. (CSCW 2019) ran an automated crawl of roughly 11,000 shopping sites and about 53,000 product pages, and found 1,818 dark-pattern instances spanning 15 types in 7 categories on 1,254 of the sites (about 11 percent), 183 of which used outright deceptive practices, plus 22 third-party entities offering dark patterns as a turnkey solution (research evidence). That paper converted the field from a collection of screenshots into a measured prevalence claim, and it is the paper every later academic and regulatory source cites for scale.

Two years after that, Mathur, Mayer and Kshirsagar (CHI 2021) pointed out that naming and counting patterns still left the field without an agreed answer to the harder question: why is any of this wrong. They proposed judging a design against four normative lenses, individual welfare, collective welfare, regulatory objectives and individual autonomy, and argued that a taxonomy without a normative argument attached to it is a list, not a critique. This card's own four-lens table below folds regulatory objectives into the regulation map that runs alongside it, so its "normative lens" row narrows the paper's four to the three that are about the person rather than the regulator; that narrowing is this card's choice, not the paper's.

Regulators built a third vocabulary at the same time, each shaped by what that regulator already had power over: the EU folded it into platform-interface law (the Digital Services Act), California folded it into consent and request-method rules (the CCPA regulations), the US FTC folded it into its existing unfair-and-deceptive-practices authority (a 2022 staff report, then individual enforcement orders), and the UK's Competition and Markets Authority framed it as a competition and consumer-harm question under the name online choice architecture. These readings are not independent of each other or of the academic lineage: the FTC's 2022 staff report cites the CMA's online choice architecture paper repeatedly and also draws on a European Commission behavioural study, and the CMA paper itself reviews prior work from the European Commission and national regulators including the Dutch ACM and Sweden's Konsumentverket. The regulators drew on each other and on the same academic literature, then framed what they found through their own existing powers.

## When to use it

- A growth or retention flow narrows a choice: an upsell, a subscription, a downgrade, a consent banner, a cancellation flow.
- Release readiness needs a choice-symmetry pass before a launch or a flow change ships, per [Gate 5](../../os/STAGE-GATES.md).
- A stakeholder disputes whether a flow is "just good conversion design," and the review needs a shared vocabulary before it needs a lawyer.
- A compliance-impact assessment has flagged a consent, request or cancellation flow and needs the design vocabulary that names what the flow is actually doing.

**Skip it when** the screen presents no decision at all (a status page, a confirmation the user cannot act on) or the review has already reached counsel and needs a legal determination rather than a design vocabulary; that determination is counsel's or a regulator's to make, with [reg-gap-check](../../skills/reg-gap-check/SKILL.md) and the compliance-impact assessment routing the question to them, not to this card.

## Four lenses

No single taxonomy in this field does every job a review needs, because each one was built to answer a different question. Run a flow through all four, in this order, rather than stopping at the first one that produces a name.

| Lens | Source | Answers | Unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Surface pattern name | Brignull, deceptive.design, 18 named types (practitioner heuristic): sneaking, forced action, hard to cancel, preselection, obstruction, hidden subscription, hidden costs, trick wording, visual interference, fake social proof, fake urgency, nagging, fake scarcity, disguised ads, confirmshaming, comparison prevention, addictive design, currency confusion | What does a person actually see on this screen | A name for the shape |
| Mechanism group | UK CMA, Online Choice Architecture discussion paper (April 2022), 21 named practices grouped as choice structure, choice information and choice pressure (practitioner heuristic; several practices marked as almost always harmful) | Which part of the interface produces the effect: the layout and defaults, the information shown or withheld, or the pressure applied | Where in the flow to look |
| Harm group | FTC, Bringing Dark Patterns to Light (2022 staff report), four groups: inducing false beliefs, hiding or delaying disclosure of material information, leading to unauthorized charges, obscuring or subverting privacy choices (practitioner heuristic: an enforcement agency's synthesis of its own case history) | What actually goes wrong for the person if the pattern works | Severity, for prioritizing a fix |
| Normative lens | Mathur, Mayer and Kshirsagar (CHI 2021) name four lenses: individual welfare, collective welfare, regulatory objectives and individual autonomy (research evidence). This row runs the first, second and fourth here, since the regulation map below already carries regulatory objectives as its own pass | Why the pattern is wrong, argued rather than asserted | The case a reviewer has to make, not just the label they attach |

A worked example, ILLUSTRATIVE: Tandem, the invented freelance-contract tool from [jobs to be done](../jobs-to-be-done.md), adds a Pro add-on that upgrades in one click but only downgrades through a phone call. Surface name: hard to cancel, Brignull's own term for easy to sign up, very hard to cancel. Mechanism group: choice structure, because the asymmetry lives in the number of steps and the channel switch, not in what information is shown. Harm group: unauthorized charges, because a customer who cannot reach support before the renewal date is billed again. Normative lens: autonomy, because the design substitutes friction for the customer's actual preference. Four lenses, one screen, four different questions answered.

## The symmetry principle

Strip away each source's own vocabulary and one testable rule survives across all of them: the path away from a business's preferred outcome must cost no more than the path toward it.

- **DSA recital 67 and Article 25.** Article 25(1) bars online-platform providers from designing, organizing or operating an interface in a way that deceives or manipulates users, or materially distorts or impairs their ability to make free and informed decisions. Article 25(2) excludes practices already governed by the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive or the GDPR, so the article covers a narrower slice of online-platform interfaces than "every dark pattern" might suggest. Article 25(3) empowers the European Commission to issue guidelines naming specific asymmetries: giving one option more prominence than another, repeatedly requesting a choice a user has already made, and making cancellation harder than subscribing was.
- **CCPA regulations, 11 CCR 7004(a)(2) and 7004(b).** For methods of submitting a CCPA request or giving consent, the more privacy-protective choice must not be longer, harder or more time-consuming to take than the less protective one. 7004(b) provides that a business's use of a method that does not comply with 7004(a) can be treated as a dark pattern, and agreement obtained through a dark pattern does not constitute consent under the CCPA; 7004(c) treats the business's intent as a factor, never as determinative. [OPEN: 7004 was amended in the CPPA's 2025 rulemaking package, effective 1 Jan 2026 (approved by OAL 22 Sep 2025); re-verify (a)(2), (b) and (c) against the text effective that date at cppa.ca.gov before citing]
- **The FTC's Amazon order (25 September 2025).** Amazon settled FTC claims over Prime enrollment and cancellation for $2.5 billion total, a $1 billion civil penalty and $1.5 billion returned to consumers (regulatory record; source in Reading below). The order requires a clear and conspicuous decline option at enrollment, bars decline wording framed as if the customer is forfeiting a named benefit, requires the material terms to be disclosed before enrollment, and requires cancellation to be possible through the same method the customer used to sign up. Three independent instruments, three different legal bases, one shared rule: symmetry is a design requirement now, not a courtesy.

## Effect, not intent

DSA recital 67 defines a dark pattern as a practice that materially distorts or impairs a user's autonomous, informed choice, "either on purpose or in effect." The CPPA's Enforcement Advisory 2024-02 states the blunt version of the same position: dark patterns are about effect, not intent. CCPA regulation 11 CCR 7004(c) is softer than the advisory: a business's intent counts as one factor but does not decide the question on its own, and the regulation separately says that knowing a method has a harmful effect and not remedying it counts as evidence of a dark pattern regardless of the original intent. The advisory adds a specific consequence a PM should know before it becomes a surprise: a business is accountable for a dark pattern its own consent-management vendor deploys on its behalf, so "the platform we bought does that, not us" is not a defense any of these sources recognize.

The practical consequence for a review: lead with effect; stated intent never excuses a measured effect. Ask what the interface does to the choices people actually end up making, measured the way the symmetry principle measures it.

## Terminology is contested

Brignull's own site no longer uses the term "dark patterns," having renamed itself deceptive.design; "dark" as a metaphor for harmful drew fair criticism unrelated to the patterns themselves. Regulators split further: the DSA, the CCPA and the FTC all still say "dark patterns" in their own text, while the UK's CMA frames the same territory as online choice architecture, its own wider name for the space, covering design that is neutral or beneficial as well as harmful, with a named subset of practices marked as almost always harmful; the CMA's paper itself notes that these concerns also go by the names dark patterns and sludge. Mathur, Mayer and Kshirsagar's own 2021 argument is that the field lacks an agreed normative definition beneath the shared name, which is a stronger claim than a naming dispute: two people can use the identical term and mean different underlying tests. Not every named pattern commands agreement either. Aggressive but honest marketing sits close enough to categories such as fake urgency or nagging that a flow can be described with the same word a reviewer would use for outright deception, so naming a pattern is the start of an argument, never the end of one.

## No hall of shame

This card names exactly one real company in a negative light: Amazon, and only through the FTC's own public settlement order, a document anyone can read at the source link in Reading below. That is not a licence to extend the habit. When a review needs an example of a pattern this card names, reach for an invented product labeled ILLUSTRATIVE, the same discipline this repository's [examples](../../examples/README.md) use throughout, rather than a screenshot of a real competitor's flow. Naming a real company from a screenshot, a guess, or a news article that is not itself a public enforcement record risks defamation and adds nothing a regulator's own words could not say more safely.

## The trap: a name is not a finding

Citing a Brignull type or a CMA mechanism group and stopping there reads exactly like a completed review and is not one. Naming the pattern is lens one of four, and none of the four lenses alone, nor all four together, is a legal determination; only counsel or a regulator makes that call, working from evidence a PM's review can supply but never substitute for. The tell: a review comment that names a pattern, cites no step count, names no jurisdiction, and argues no harm. That comment has spent thirty seconds producing the feeling of rigor.

The second version of the same trap is malicious compliance with the letter of the symmetry rule. A team reads "declining must cost the same as accepting" and makes accepting slower instead of making declining faster, satisfying the literal step count while leaving the product worse for everyone. The symmetry principle is a floor stated relative to the other path, not an absolute usability bar, and a review that only counts steps without asking whether either path is reasonable will pass a flow that technically complies and still frustrates every person who touches it.

## How it lies

The whole field's honest weakness is that naming is not measuring. A pattern name describes what a screen looks like; it says nothing about whether the screen changed anyone's decision. Mathur et al.'s own contribution was to move the field from naming toward counting, and even a count of instances across a crawl is a count of screens, not a count of decisions actually altered. A rigorous review needs behavioral evidence, a funnel comparison, an A/B result, an actual complaint, and the four lenses above do not supply it; they only tell you where to look for it.

The regulatory map lies by omission if read as a complete net. Every instrument named here is narrower than "every dark pattern everywhere": the DSA only reaches online platforms and explicitly excludes practices the UCPD or GDPR already cover; the CCPA rule only reaches request and consent methods; the FTC's order binds one company under one set of facts. A flow can clear every named instrument in the regulation map and still be predatory in a way none of them individually reaches, because the map is a union of narrow slices, not a general prohibition.

The four taxonomies also disagree with each other in shape, not just in vocabulary. Brignull organizes by what a person sees, the CMA by which interface mechanism produces the effect, the FTC by what harm follows, and Mathur 2021 by why it is wrong. The same real screen can land in different boxes depending which lens a reviewer picked first, and "which category is this" is frequently a judgment call dressed as a lookup.

## Where it sits in the loop

- Stage: DELIVER, at [Gate 5: release readiness green](../../os/STAGE-GATES.md), where a choice-symmetry pass belongs on the readiness checklist for any flow that changed a consent, cancellation, upgrade or downgrade path.
- Upstream: the flow itself is usually designed earlier, at DEFINE in [design brief](../../templates/definition/design-brief.md) or during a compliance-impact assessment opened at DEFINE, so a symmetry problem caught here is a problem that should have been caught before Gate 5, not a substitute for catching it then.
- Downstream: the [choice-symmetry audit](../../frameworks/design/choice-symmetry-audit.md) worksheet runs the actual step count per flow, tagged against all four lenses and a jurisdiction column, and feeds the [compliance impact assessment](../../templates/operate/compliance-impact-assessment.md) where a regulator applies. Its finding is meant to land as a row on [release readiness](../../templates/delivery/release-readiness.md) before a GO, NO-GO or CONDITIONAL GO decision.
- Handed to counsel through [reg-gap-check](../../skills/reg-gap-check/SKILL.md) the moment a finding needs a legal determination rather than a design vocabulary; this card supplies the vocabulary and the evidence, never the ruling.
