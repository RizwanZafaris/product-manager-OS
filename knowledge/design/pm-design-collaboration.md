# PM and Design Collaboration

Based on Sarah Gibbons's critique versus review distinction (Nielsen Norman Group, 2016) and Scott Berkun's design critique practice (2003), read alongside Nolwenn Maudet and colleagues' study of designer and developer handoff (ACM CSCW 2017), Kurt Luther and colleagues' study of structured crowd critique (ACM CSCW 2015), the GitLab product design handbook, Teresa Torres's product trio writing at Product Talk, and Intercom's own account of its problem first process. Explained here in this repository's own words.

## The essence

A product manager sits beside experience design at every stage without becoming the designer. The single hardest thing to get right in that relationship is knowing which conversation is happening in the room: improving a piece of work, or approving it. Sarah Gibbons's 2016 article at Nielsen Norman Group draws the line precisely. A design critique "refers to analyzing a design, and giving feedback on whether it meets its objectives," and is run as a standalone gathering with the sole purpose of improving a particular piece of work. A design review is a different event: an evaluation of a design against a set of heuristics, usually held to gain approval and move the work forward. Those two events get run in the same meeting more often than anyone in the room would admit, and the confusion produces exactly the failure both were built to prevent. Work gets waved through because everyone thought they were only critiquing it and nobody wanted to be the one to say no. Or a critique's improving energy curdles into a gatekeeping fight because someone in the room believed a decision was actually being made.

This card is the product side of that relationship: how a critique stays a critique instead of drifting into either extreme, what a measured study of real handoffs actually found breaks between a designer and a developer, where design debt gets logged instead of relitigated at every planning meeting, and the one decision rights question the design literature has not settled between itself, so this repository states its own answer and names exactly where that answer came from.

## Where it came from

Gibbons's critique versus review split formalizes a distinction design studios have practiced informally for decades: Scott Berkun's essay on running a design critique predates it by thirteen years, written from his own practice leading design reviews at Microsoft, and the practical advice in both pieces largely agrees despite the different vantage points. The academic wing sits apart from both: Kurt Luther and colleagues built a web based system, CrowdCrit, to test whether critique from many non expert crowd workers could be structured and aggregated into something as useful as a design studio's own critique culture, and Nolwenn Maudet and colleagues ran interviews, observed live handoff meetings, and ran a participatory design session specifically to find where a handoff between a designer and a developer actually breaks, rather than where practitioners assume it breaks.

The decision rights half of this card comes from a live disagreement inside product management writing. Teresa Torres's Product Talk essays on the product trio argue that a product manager, a designer, and an engineer are jointly responsible and accountable for every risk a product carries. GitLab's public handbook, and Marty Cagan's earlier writing at Silicon Valley Product Group, describe something closer to a seat owning its risk: a directly responsible individual for design decisions, a directly responsible individual for engineering decisions. This repository is built on Cagan's four risk framing already, in [empowered product teams](../cagan-product-teams.md), so it cannot simply pick Torres's side and walk away from its own foundation. [Triad Decision Rights](../roles/triad-decision-rights.md) is this repository's answer to that tension, and this card names the dispute it answers.

## When to use it

- Setting the ground rules before a design critique runs, so the room knows in advance whether it is improving the work or approving it.
- Reviewing acceptance criteria or a handoff spec against Maudet's three breakdown types, before a build starts rather than after it ships wrong.
- A stakeholder disputes who gets the final call on a design decision, and the argument is happening for the first time in the middle of a deadline.
- Logging a known, deliberate rough edge in the experience so it gets tracked and repaid rather than argued about again next quarter.
- Deciding how much of a DesignOps proposal is actually the product manager's decision to make.

**Skip it when:** the work in front of you is a one screen bug fix with an obvious correct answer. Convening a critique, debating decision rights, or opening the tech debt register over a single misaligned button spends more process than the fix is worth; just make the fix and move on.

## Critique against review

Gibbons names two roles inside a critique: the presenter, who shares the design, and the critiquer, who offers informed feedback, and either role can hold more than one person. Three habits keep a critique a critique rather than letting it slide into an ad hoc review: a clear scope for what is and is not up for discussion, an objective everyone in the room has actually agreed the design is trying to meet, and conversation rather than command, since a directive shuts down exactly the open discussion a critique exists to produce. When any of the three is missing, feedback becomes subjective, because there is no shared target to measure the design against.

The review half of the pair is not this card's method. Where a critique produces improving feedback with no decision attached, the decision itself, and the record of who signed it, belongs to [Design Review Record](../../templates/architecture/design-review-record.md) at Gate 3. Keeping the two apart in the calendar, not only in definition, is what stops a critique from quietly becoming the review nobody scheduled.

## Running a critique that stays a critique

Berkun's practical shape for a critique meeting is specific enough to run from. A critique seats three to seven people: fewer than that and the room lacks range, more than that and it stops being informal enough for honest, quick feedback. Someone leads it, and that leader states three or four concrete questions the group is there to answer; without stated questions, a critique becomes a brainstorm wearing a critique's name, because everyone in the room works from a different unstated assumption about what they are there to judge.

The questions Berkun recommends are altitude dependent, and the altitude should shift as the project moves forward. Early in a project, a critique should weigh the higher level user, customer, and business goals, and hold off on eliminating an idea over an engineering concern or an aesthetic preference, flagging the concern rather than killing the idea outright. As the timeline progresses, the pressure for solved issues rises and the bar for entertaining a brand new idea rises with it; a critique's scope should peak during planning and steadily narrow from there. A question that falls outside the meeting's stated scope does not get argued in the room either way: it gets written down and taken outside the meeting, which is the practice the [Design Critique](../../frameworks/design/design-critique.md) worksheet turns into its own parking lot column.

## Why a structured critique outperforms an unstructured one

Luther and colleagues' 2015 study, read here at abstract level, tested whether critique gathered from a crowd of non expert contributors, structured and aggregated rather than left as a pile of individual comments, could compare with the design studio's own expert critique. The aggregated, structured approach compared favorably against the expert baseline; designers who received it reported that it improved their design process, and were enthusiastic enough about the feedback to actually change their designs because of it. The finding that matters for this card is not about crowds specifically: it is that structure and aggregation, not individual expertise alone, is doing real work in producing feedback a designer will actually act on. A critique run against Berkun's stated questions and scope, with feedback consolidated rather than left scattered across a dozen unrelated comments, is applying the same mechanism inside a single team's room.

A modern design tool's own handoff features are named in this card only as an example of what a status looks like once it exists, never as something this repository's gates depend on. Figma's Dev Mode, for instance, lets a team mark a design as ready for development in a dedicated view, with a status that records when the design last changed since handoff; that specific feature sits behind an Organization or Enterprise plan tier. No template or gate in this repository names a tool feature as a requirement, because a tool feature that only some plans carry cannot be a gate condition every team can clear; what the [Design Review Record](../../templates/architecture/design-review-record.md) checks is the underlying behavior, a handoff status that exists and is current, not a specific button in a specific product.

## The handoff gap

Maudet and colleagues ran three studies to find out where a handoff between a designer and a developer actually breaks, rather than assume it from either side's complaints. Sixteen interviews found that current handoff practice induces unnecessary rework and produces discrepancies between the original design and what gets implemented, and the study names three specific breakdown types: a designer omits a critical detail, a designer ignores the presence of an edge case, or a designer disregards a technical limitation the implementation is actually working under. [Design Review Record](../../templates/architecture/design-review-record.md) turns those three named breakdowns into a checklist in its own handoff section, rather than leaving handoff quality as a vague feeling that the spec was thorough.

The study's second finding is the one teams most often miss: handoff breakdown is recurring, not a one time event a good kickoff meeting solves. Maudet and colleagues observed two live meetings between designers and developers and found that early developer involvement did mitigate some potential breakdowns, but new breakdowns kept emerging as the project unfolded past that first meeting. A change log since handoff, which the review record carries forward into its own section, exists because of this finding specifically: the gap reopens every time the design or the build changes, not only at the moment of the first handoff.

## Design QA on the live build

GitLab's publicly documented practice for its product designers requires them to review and approve any merge request carrying a user facing change, defined broadly enough to include both a visual change of any size and a change to the rendered page that would affect what a screen reader announces. The handbook's own instruction to the reviewer is specific: always preview the merge request in a live environment, because screenshots and recorded video do not show everything a hover state, a focus state, or an actual assistive technology pass would reveal. That is design QA on the running build rather than on the design file the build was supposed to match, and it is exactly the distinction [Design Review Record](../../templates/architecture/design-review-record.md) makes structural by splitting itself across two gates: a Gate 3 record built from the design artifact, and a separate live build section, completed once BUILD produces something to actually walk, that a Gate 4 sign off checks against the running product rather than a screenshot of it.

The licence on GitLab's handbook prose is not settled cleanly enough to adapt it directly: the handbook repository reports an MIT licence, but that licence's own text scopes itself only to the GitLab Software rather than to the handbook's published prose, leaving the prose's own status ambiguous. What is reused here is the practice, paraphrased and attributed, not the handbook's own wording.

## Decision rights: the dispute this repository states rather than hides

Two credible sources in this lane disagree with each other, and pretending they agree would be worse than naming the disagreement. Teresa Torres argues, in her Product Talk essay answering exactly this question, that every member of the product trio is responsible and accountable for every risk area: desirability, viability, feasibility, usability, and ethics all at once, because splitting risks into individual ownership sets the engineer and the designer against each other the moment the most usable answer is not the most feasible one. GitLab's handbook describes the opposite shape in practice: a product manager owns the what and the why, a product designer owns how the direction is experienced and is named the directly responsible individual for design decisions, and an engineer owns how the product gets built. Marty Cagan's earlier writing at Silicon Valley Product Group, an essay now two decades old and cited here only for its historical framing of design as a set of distinct professional roles a product manager collaborates with rather than performs, sits closer to the seat ownership camp than to Torres's joint account.

This repository does not resolve that dispute by picking a side. [Triad Decision Rights](../roles/triad-decision-rights.md) states a third position, built on Cagan's own four risk framing: each seat carries a first voice on the risk closest to its craft, meaning its call stands once evidence runs out, not that it is the only voice permitted to speak on that risk. A first voice owes the others a testable question, framed as an opening to explore rather than a flat no, and a genuine dispute runs a stated three step path: restate it as a falsifiable disagreement, buy the cheapest evidence that would settle it, and let the first voice decide if the evidence cannot arrive in time, with the decision and the dissent both recorded rather than one side simply winning by holding the room longer. That is neither Torres's joint account nor GitLab's clean seat ownership; it borrows the seat structure from one and the standing obligation to answer, not merely to veto, from the other.

## Problem before solution

Intercom's own account of its product culture opens with a sentence its writers say gets used across product reviews, roadmap meetings, and design critiques alike: naming the problem before naming a solution. Their onboarding materials call a written problem statement an Intermission, and Intercom's own retrospective on how it allocates a team's effort claims that, compared with a company that spends most of its time designing and building a solution before testing it against customers, Intercom spends roughly a third of a team's total effort on problem prioritization and problem definition before any solution design work begins.

That figure is a piece of self description, not a benchmark this repository asks any team to hit. Intercom's own writing says explicitly that there is no fixed amount of time a team should spend defining a problem, from an hour to ten weeks, and that the right amount depends on the problem's severity and how many customers it touches. What the figure is useful for is naming the shape of the mistake it is arguing against: a critique or a review convened before the room agrees what problem the design is actually solving has nothing real to check the design against, which is exactly Gibbons's second theme above, an agreed upon objective, stated as a specific failure mode rather than a generic reminder to be diligent.

## UX debt: tracked, not relitigated

Nielsen Norman Group's Anna Kaley describes UX debt as the experience side of technical debt, a concept originally coined by Ward Cunningham in 1992: the accumulated cost of choosing the faster or easier design solution over the better one, repaid later with interest because fixing a shipped problem costs more than building it correctly the first time would have. Kaley names concrete causes worth checking a team's own process against: skipping user testing, disregarding an established style guide, design by committee, a misread of the product's own vision, the seams left by an acquisition or a merger, poor documentation, insufficient QA, and legacy code nobody has refactored.

This repository draws a line UX debt discussions routinely blur. Deliberate debt, a known rough edge the team decided to ship because the alternative cost more than the team could spend that sprint, belongs on the [Tech Debt Register](../../templates/execution/tech-debt-register.md) with an interest figure attached, exactly like a technical debt item, because it was a decision made in the open and it is owed a plan to repay. An A/B test arm is not debt at all, even when the losing variant is deliberately rougher than the winner: nobody intends to repay a losing arm, because it is disposable by design, and only the variant that wins graduates into the product the register would ever need to track.

## DesignOps: a menu, mostly outside product scope

Kate Kaplan defines DesignOps, at Nielsen Norman Group, as the practice of orchestrating and optimizing a design team's people, processes, and craft so that design's own value and impact scale with the organization around it, covering a menu that runs from hiring and team growth through tooling and workflow efficiency to the quality and impact of what a design team ships. Most of that menu sits with a design leader's own staffing, budget, and tooling decisions, not with product management, and this card does not attempt to relitigate it as PM scope. The one slice of the menu that does touch a product manager's own process is the part that intersects a critique's cadence and a handoff's checkpoints, both of which this card already covers directly through [Design Critique](../../frameworks/design/design-critique.md) and [Design Review Record](../../templates/architecture/design-review-record.md); a proposal that reaches further than that into headcount or tooling budget belongs to the design leader who owns DesignOps, cited here rather than adopted.

## The trap: feedback at the wrong altitude

The single most common way a product manager wrecks a critique is speaking at the wrong altitude for the phase the project is actually in, in either direction. Early, when Berkun's own guidance says the room should be weighing user, customer, and business goals, a product manager who fixates on a specific spacing value or a specific word choice drags the whole room down to a level of detail nobody there is positioned to evaluate yet, and buries the higher level question the critique actually needed answered. Late, once the timeline has moved past planning and the bar for new ideas has properly risen per Berkun's own phase guidance, a product manager who reopens the fundamental direction of the design is relitigating a decision the room already made, at the cost of the specific, buildable feedback the meeting was actually convened to collect.

Naming this failure with the word design communities use for it invites exactly the reaction that shuts a critique down before it starts, so this card does not use that word. What matters operationally is smaller and checkable: before speaking, a product manager should be able to say which of Berkun's altitude bands, business and user goals, or implementation detail, the current phase calls for, and hold comments to that band. A comment that cannot be traced to one of the design brief's stated objectives is a taste opinion wearing feedback's clothes, and belongs in the parking lot the critique worksheet already carries, not in the room's live discussion.

## How it lies: an approved record is not a working product

A signed Gate 3 design review record looks like proof the experience works. What it actually proves, on its own, is that the design artifact, a set of screens, a prototype, a set of specified states, passed review against a set of stated objectives at one point in time. Maudet's own finding is the reason that gap matters rather than being a technicality: new breakdowns kept appearing as a real project moved past its first handoff meeting, which means a review record built from a design file is checking the file, not the thing that eventually ships from it. That is precisely why [Design Review Record](../../templates/architecture/design-review-record.md) is built across two gates rather than one, with its own live build section left empty until BUILD produces something to actually walk, and GitLab's own reviewing habit, always preview the merge request in a live environment, is the practice that section exists to formalize. A record signed only at Gate 3, with the live build section never completed, is a record of the mockup, not of the product, however complete its earlier sections read.

## Where it sits in the loop

- Stage: ALL STAGES. Unlike a card tied to one gate's checklist, critique, review, handoff, and decision rights disputes recur every time a product decision reaches a screen, from a DEFINE stage brief's first sketches through a BUILD stage live build check.
- Upstream: the objectives a critique checks a design against come from [design brief](../../templates/definition/design-brief.md); the triad itself, and the four risk framing this card's decision rights section is built on, come from [empowered product teams](../cagan-product-teams.md).
- Runs as: [Design Critique](../../frameworks/design/design-critique.md), the worksheet form of Gibbons's and Berkun's practice, scoped, timed, and closed with an outcome log rather than an approval.
- Feeds: [Design Review Record](../../templates/architecture/design-review-record.md) carries the approval a critique never makes, first at Gate 3 against the design artifact and again, in its live build section, at Gate 4 against the running product; deliberate UX debt a critique or a review surfaces is logged in the [Tech Debt Register](../../templates/execution/tech-debt-register.md) rather than argued over again at the next planning meeting.
- On trial at: [Gate 3: architecture and risks reviewed](../../os/STAGE-GATES.md#gate-3-architecture-and-risks-reviewed) and [Gate 4: acceptance criteria met](../../os/STAGE-GATES.md#gate-4-acceptance-criteria-met), through what the review record signs at each, never at this card directly, since a critique itself produces no decision for a gate to check.
