# UX Measurement

Based on John Brooke's System Usability Scale chapter (Redhatch Consulting Ltd., written for Digital Equipment Co., in *Usability Evaluation in Industry*, 1996), James R. Lewis, Brian S. Utesch and Deborah E. Maher's UMUX-LITE paper (IBM, CHI 2013), Jeff Sauro and Joseph S. Dumas's comparison of one-question post-task measures (CHI 2009), and Kerry Rodden, Hilary Hutchinson and Xin Fu's HEART framework (Google, CHI 2010). Explained here in this repository's own words, for measuring experience design once people are actually using the thing; never a description of the DESIGN stage or its Gate 3.

## The essence

Usability measurement has three altitudes, and the instrument that answers one question is the wrong instrument for the other two. At the task level, a person either completed the thing or did not, in some time, with some errors, and left a one item reaction behind; that is evidence about one screen or one flow. At the test level, a whole session with a whole prototype produces one composite number, a System Usability Scale (SUS) score or its short alternative, that lets one version be compared with another or with a competitor; that is evidence about a design, not about any single task inside it. At the product level, a shipped feature is watched over weeks and months through Happiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention and Task success (HEART); that is evidence about whether the thing keeps earning its place, which a single session can never show. A number produced at one altitude does not substitute for a number owed at another: a glowing SUS score after one test session says nothing about whether adoption holds up in month two, and a strong retention curve says nothing about whether the interface itself is easy to use.

Two boundaries keep this card from swallowing work that belongs elsewhere. A heuristic score, the tally an expert produces by walking a screen against Nielsen's or Shneiderman's list, is an inspection method's count of suspected problems, not a measurement of experience; it estimates where to look, and only a person actually using the thing produces a usability number. And a Net Promoter Score is not a usability measure: it asks about loyalty and likelihood to recommend, a different construct that happens to correlate with usability, at .74 and .73 across two samples in the UMUX-LITE research, research evidence, not identity with it. A recommend-likelihood number and a usability number can move in opposite directions on the same release, and a readout that quotes one to stand for the other is quietly answering a question nobody asked.

## Where it came from

The System Usability Scale was built at Digital Equipment Co. in the 1980s for a specific, unglamorous constraint: usability engineers running travelling lab sessions with customers who had just spent twenty minutes to an hour struggling through evaluation exercises, and who would not sit through a twenty-five item questionnaire afterward. Brooke's team pooled fifty candidate statements, had twenty staff rate two contrasting systems, one judged easy and one judged nearly unusable, against all fifty, and kept the ten items that discriminated most sharply between the two, alternating positive and negative tone so a respondent has to read each item rather than mark a column on autopilot. The scale sits downstream of the ISO 9241-11 triad, effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction, that the same research programme was formalising at the time; SUS became the fast, general-purpose way to capture the satisfaction leg of that triad without a bespoke instrument for every study.

UMUX-LITE arrived at IBM in 2013 for the opposite constraint: teams that found even ten items too many when a satisfaction question was one line inside a much larger survey. It descends from an earlier four-item instrument, UMUX, that a parallel-analysis critique had shown was probably measuring two things rather than one; Lewis, Utesch and Maher kept the two items that loaded most cleanly onto usability and validated the pair across two independent surveys. Sauro and Dumas's 2009 study sits slightly to the side of both: rather than proposing a new scale, it compared three single-question, post-task measures head to head, a plain Likert item, Usability Magnitude Estimation, and the Subjective Mental Effort Question (SMEQ), to see which was worth asking after every task inside a session. HEART is the outlier by origin: built at Google for web applications with enough traffic that behavioural signals are cheap and abundant, it answers a problem the other three do not touch, which metric to trust when a team has plenty of data and no discipline about what it means.

## When to use it

Reach for the task level whenever a usability test is running; it is the raw material of [the usability test plan](../../templates/discovery/usability-test-plan.md), sections 3 and 4. Reach for the test level, SUS or UMUX-LITE, whenever a session needs one comparable number: a before-and-after on a redesign, a competitive benchmark, a stakeholder question that boils down to "is this better." Reach for the product level, HEART's happiness category run beside its behavioural categories, whenever a shipped feature needs an ongoing definition of success, which is what `frameworks/metrics/heart-metrics.md` turns into a metrics-dictionary entry.

**Skip it when:** nobody downstream will act on the number. A scorecard collected because a template has a row for it, then filed, is not measurement; it is a ritual wearing measurement's clothes. Skip the test-level composite specifically when a round has too few participants to trust a proportion, the qualitative findings from that same round are worth more than a computed score, and reporting the number invites a false precision the sample cannot support; see the trap below.

## Measuring at the task level

Four things, collected inside the same session, at the smallest grain: whether the task succeeded, unassisted completion counted separately from an assisted one; how long it took, read against a max time agreed before the round; how many errors occurred, recovered or not; and a single post-task reaction, asked right after the task while it is still fresh. The first three already have fields in [the usability test plan](../../templates/discovery/usability-test-plan.md)'s task and success-criteria tables; the fourth is what Sauro and Dumas's 2009 study is actually about. They ran twenty-six participants through two applications and asked a plain Likert item, Usability Magnitude Estimation, and SMEQ after each task; all three told the two applications apart, research evidence, but the plain Likert item and SMEQ were more sensitive with a small sample and considerably easier to administer than Usability Magnitude Estimation's open-ended numeric estimate.

That plain Likert item is the direct ancestor of what later MeasuringU writing named and benchmarked as the Single Ease Question (SEQ). That later naming and its published benchmark numbers were not among the sources read for this card, so no SEQ benchmark is stated here; ask a post-task ease item, expect it to discriminate the way Sauro and Dumas found, and do not quote a specific SEQ norm until that later material has actually been read.

## Measuring at the test level: SUS and UMUX-LITE

SUS is ten statements, alternating positive and negative tone, each answered on a five point agreement scale. Scoring, in this repository's own words rather than Brooke's: for the five positively worded items the contribution is the scale position minus one; for the five negatively worded items the contribution is five minus the scale position; every contribution now runs zero to four, sum the ten, and multiply by two and a half to land a score from zero to one hundred, standard, the scale's own published procedure. Brooke is explicit that scores for individual items are not meaningful on their own, only the composite; a "4" on the complexity item, already reverse coded into that four, says nothing by itself about the product, and a readout that walks through item-by-item scores is reading noise as if it were ten separate findings. The instrument itself, per Brooke's chapter, has been made freely available for usability assessment, on the condition that a published report acknowledges the source of the measure, which is why the items are linked to the primary source here rather than reproduced in full: this card's own house rule caps every file at one short quote, and that quote is spent below on UMUX-LITE instead.

UMUX-LITE cuts to two items. One, quoted with attribution because the paper offers it as the instrument itself: "This system is easy to use" (Lewis, Utesch and Maher, 2013). The second item asks, in the same paper, whether the system's capabilities meet the respondent's stated requirements; read it at the source rather than here. Across two independent survey samples the pair showed reliability of .82 and .83, research evidence, excellent for a two-item instrument, and correlated with SUS scores at .81 in both samples. That correlation is strong enough to make UMUX-LITE a working stand-in for SUS when ten items are genuinely too many, but the paper also found that raw UMUX-LITE means run slightly lower than SUS means for the same systems; comparing a raw UMUX-LITE number against an SUS benchmark, without applying the paper's own linear regression adjustment first, understates the product and is a second, quieter way this pairing misleads. See how it lies, below.

A generic SUS benchmark of around 68, standard deviation about 12.5, is widely cited as the average across studies; treat it as a historical average, not a pass mark, because it is pooled across products and contexts that Brooke's own chapter says should not be compared to one another directly. A meta-analysis of digital health apps found a mean of 76.64, standard deviation 15.12, with a skewed, non-normal distribution, research evidence from Hyzy et al., 2022, evidence that the generic number does not transfer to every domain. A domain-matched baseline, not the generic 68, is the number a real comparison needs.

## Measuring at the product level: HEART

Product-level measurement is happiness, the attitude category HEART shares in spirit with SUS and UMUX-LITE, run beside engagement, adoption, retention and task success, the behavioural categories that a single test session cannot see because they only exist over time. The full goals-signals-metrics procedure, with its worked worksheet, lives in `frameworks/metrics/heart-metrics.md`; this card supplies the measurement instruments that worksheet's happiness row and task-success row actually call.

Report assistive-technology users and right-to-left locale users as their own segments in every product-level cut, never folded into the aggregate. A HEART dashboard that is healthy on average can be hiding a screen-reader flow that fails every time, or a mirrored layout that nobody who reads left to right would ever notice; averaging across a segment that experiences a materially different product erases the one signal that would have caught it. The same discipline applies one level down: a test-level SUS round run only with participants who do not use assistive technology has measured one product, not the product every customer receives.

## The trap: a single number with no n and no baseline

A "72" dropped into a launch readout, with no participant count beside it and no domain-matched baseline to read it against, looks like evidence and is closer to a rumor with two significant figures. The reader cannot tell whether it came from thirty participants or three, and cannot tell whether 72 is strong, weak, or exactly average, because "average" itself depends on the domain: 68 for the pooled generic benchmark, 76.64 for health apps, and an unstated number for everything else, research evidence from Hyzy et al., 2022 showing how much that generic number moves once a domain is actually studied. The fix costs one sentence: state the participant count next to the score, and name the baseline the score is being read against, generic or domain-matched, every time the number is spoken aloud outside the room that collected it.

## How it lies

Its most reliable distortion is retrospective, not statistical. The aesthetic-usability effect, the tendency for an attractive interface to be rated easier to use than it measurably is, and the peak-end rule, the tendency for a retrospective rating to be dominated by the most intense moment and the ending rather than by the average of the whole experience, both inflate a satisfaction number collected after the fact relative to what actually happened during the session. Neither effect touches task success, error count, or time on task, because those are observed rather than remembered. That asymmetry is the argument for ranking task success above satisfaction in a readout, not the other way around: a product can be genuinely hard to use and still be rated pleasant to have used, and a launch decision built on the pleasant number alone inherits a bias the harder numbers do not carry.

The second distortion is the one the test-level section above already named once and is worth naming again here, because it is easy to reintroduce by accident: reading a UMUX-LITE score next to an SUS benchmark without the paper's own regression correction. Because raw UMUX-LITE means sit slightly below SUS means for the same system, an uncorrected side-by-side comparison makes a UMUX-LITE product look worse than an SUS-measured one even when the underlying usability is identical. The lie is not in the instrument; it is in skipping one arithmetic step the instrument's own authors specified.

## Where it sits in the loop

- Stage: BUILD, at [Gate 4: acceptance criteria met](../../os/STAGE-GATES.md); a usability round's task-level results are part of what that gate asks to see demonstrated, not narrated by whoever built the feature.
- Upstream: usability rounds also run earlier, in DESIGN and even DISCOVER prototypes, per [the usability test plan](../../templates/discovery/usability-test-plan.md)'s own note that findings there feed Gates 1, 3 and 4 depending on when the round runs.
- Sideways: the test-level instruments in this card are what a design review reaches for when "it feels better" needs to become a number a second reviewer can check.
- Downstream: `frameworks/design/ux-scorecard.md` is where the task-level and test-level numbers from one round get assembled into a single readout; [the HEART worksheet](../../frameworks/metrics/heart-metrics.md) is where the product-level happiness category is defined precisely enough to reach a metrics dictionary and, from there, [Gate 6: outcomes verified](../../os/STAGE-GATES.md) in OPERATE.
- On trial wherever a usability number is quoted outside the room that produced it: the trap above exists because that is the moment a sourced number turns into a rumor.
