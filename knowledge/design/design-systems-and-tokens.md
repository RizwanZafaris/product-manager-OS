# Design Systems and Tokens

Based on the GOV.UK Design System's published contribution criteria and component lifecycle statuses for experience design (Government Digital Service, Open Government Licence v3.0, read September 2026), Nathan Curtis and EightShapes's team-model, contribution and versioning series (2015 to 2020), Ben Callahan and Sparkbox's design system maturity model (2018), Brad Frost's Atomic Design (2013) and design-system governance writing, the W3C Design Tokens Community Group's Format Module (2025.10), and Yassine Lamine and Jinghui Cheng's 2022 study, the only peer-reviewed research this repository's search found on the practice itself. Explained here in this repository's own words.

## The essence

A design system is not the component library a designer hands to engineering once. It is a second product, with its own users (the product teams who consume it), its own backlog, its own release cadence, and its own adoption problem, sitting above the components rather than inside any one of them. The layer this card covers is everything that makes a pile of reusable buttons survive past the first release: the rules for who may change a component and how, the tokens that let one value update a whole product's appearance instead of a thousand hand-edited files, the contract that keeps a token's name meaning the same thing in the design tool and in the shipped code, and the service-level obligations that sit above any single screen.

Two confusions are worth heading off before the rest of this card. First, craft: Usability Heuristics, Interaction Design Principles and Visual Foundations in this same shelf teach how one screen should behave; this card teaches how the organization that builds many screens keeps them consistent without a person checking every one by hand. Second, and more important for this repository specifically: when this card or its siblings say "design," they mean experience design, the craft of interfaces, never this repository's own DESIGN stage or the architecture review at [Gate 3](../../os/STAGE-GATES.md#gate-3-architecture-and-risks-reviewed). A design system decision can inform a Gate 3 review, and this card is hooked to that gate because component and token choices are exactly the kind of architectural commitment Gate 3 exists to catch, but the gate's own checklist is about system architecture and risk, not about whether a button has a hover state.

## Where it came from

Shared pattern libraries predate the phrase "design system": internal style guides and component kits existed inside large product organizations for years before anyone wrote a public methodology for running one. Two threads from the early 2010s shaped the vocabulary this card uses. Brad Frost's Atomic Design, first written up in 2013, offered a mental model for decomposing an interface into atoms, molecules, organisms, templates and pages, borrowed from chemistry as a teaching device rather than a governance framework; Frost himself is explicit that the levels are a way to think about composition, not a mandate that every team's folder structure must match them. Separately, public-sector and large commercial organizations began publishing their systems openly: the GOV.UK Design System grew out of the UK Government Digital Service's Service Standard work, USWDS, launched in 2015 and mandated for federal sites by the 21st Century IDEA Act in 2018, and Google's Material Design and IBM's Carbon gave the pattern a cross-product, commercial-scale treatment. Nathan Curtis, working as a consultant through EightShapes from the mid-2010s onward, wrote the series on team models, contribution definitions and versioning that most of this card's governance section rests on; none of that writing is peer-reviewed research, it is distilled consulting practice, stated here as such.

The academic literature on design systems as a practice is thin enough that this card can name the complete state of it: Lamine and Cheng's 2022 paper, "Understanding and Supporting the Design Systems Practice," appears to be the only peer-reviewed study of the practice itself that this repository's search turned up, as against the much larger literature on individual usability methods or accessibility standards. Their mixed-method design combined a content analysis of 4,714 GitHub issues, drawn from a random sample of 5,000 pulled out of 16,635 issues collected in June 2019 across 41 open-source design-system repositories, with semi-structured interviews of 9 design-system project leaders (three designers, three developers, three managers) conducted from August to October 2019, coded thematically with a second, blind coder and Cohen's kappa reported for inter-rater reliability. That is a real study, and it is also a small one: nine interviews is a thin base for generalizing about an entire practice, and recruiting through GitHub skews the sample toward larger, developer-facing open-source projects rather than the internal, closed-source systems most product organizations actually run. Evidence class: research evidence, with that sampling caveat attached every time this card cites it.

## When to use it

- Standing up a shared system for the first time, or deciding whether an existing one is worth the cost of keeping
- Choosing between a trial and a stable status for a new or changed component, or deciding when something stops being a special case and becomes a system feature
- Designing the token tier structure (primitive, semantic, component) before a theme or a dark mode becomes an afterthought
- Evaluating whether an adoption number a design-system team is reporting actually means what it is being used to argue
- Writing a [component spec](../../templates/architecture/component-spec.md) or negotiating the design-to-code contract with engineering before a token rename becomes a surprise breaking change
- Filling the `method` row of a [DESIGN.md](../../templates/architecture/design-md.md) or running a [design-system audit](../../frameworks/design/design-system-audit.md) against candidate foundations

**Skip it when:** the product is one product with no shared library, and no second product is credibly coming. A design system's entire value proposition is amortizing a decision across more than one consumer; with exactly one consumer, every governance question this card raises, contribution workflow, versioning policy, a token naming taxonomy, is overhead bought against a benefit that does not yet exist. Use [Visual Foundations](visual-foundations.md) and the screen-level craft cards instead, and revisit this card the day a second product actually needs the same components.

## Anatomy

Across the public systems this card draws on, GOV.UK, USWDS, Material and the NHS service manual among them, the same six-layer anatomy recurs, evidence class: platform convention, observed independently across systems built by different organizations for different governments and companies:

- **Foundations.** The design tokens themselves: color, typography, spacing, grid, motion, the raw material everything else is built from. Covered in full in the Tokens section below.
- **Components.** Individual, reusable interface elements: a button, a text input, a tag. The unit most people mean when they say "the design system," and the smallest part of what actually keeps one alive.
- **Patterns.** Compositions of components that solve a recurring problem end to end, a question page, a start page, an error-summary flow, rather than one control in isolation. NHS's information architecture names this layer explicitly, separate from its component catalog.
- **Content.** Voice, tone, and a style guide: the words a component ships with are as much a part of the system as its CSS, which is why GOV.UK's own contribution criteria require new patterns to follow the GOV.UK style guide, not only the Frontend coding standard.
- **Accessibility.** Treated as a first-class layer running through every other one rather than a separate checklist bolted on afterward; see [Accessibility and Inclusive Design](accessibility-and-inclusive-design.md) for what conformance testing against a component library does and does not prove about a composed flow.
- **Contribution.** The process layer: how a proposal becomes a component, who reviews it, and what happens when it is rejected. This is the layer most teams skip when they first build a system, and the layer whose absence is most often what the Governance section below is describing when a system fails.

Atomic Design's atoms, molecules, organisms, templates and pages sits across the foundations, components and patterns layers as a vocabulary for talking about composition, credited to Frost and used here as exactly that: a mental model a team can adopt or ignore, never a required directory structure or a governance rule. A separate, convergent finding from the component-library comparison behind Ownership Models below is worth flagging here because it answers the same anatomy question from the code side: shadcn/ui, MUI and Chakra each converge independently on the same three prop dimensions for a component, variant (appearance), size, and color or intent, plus state. Three independent lineages landing on the same three dimensions is a weak but real signal that this is closer to a real pattern than to one library's taste, in the same way three independent heuristic lists agreeing is treated in [Usability Heuristics](usability-heuristics.md).

## Top-down against bottom-up

Lamine and Cheng's interviews, described above, converged on one specific practitioner recommendation: build and evolve a design system bottom-up, eliciting and merging components out of the concrete products that already use them, rather than top-down, inventing new design ideas in isolation and imposing the result on existing products. Their participants argued that the bottom-up approach keeps the system flexible to what products actually need and eases adoption, because a component a team already helped shape feels less like an imposed migration than a distillation of work it already did. The paper frames the tension explicitly as the central challenge the practice has to manage: holding design knowledge stable enough to be worth sharing while staying flexible enough that the concrete products using it can still evolve.

Read this finding at the weight the study actually supports, not more. It is one qualitative study, nine interviewees, read through the lens of leaders who had already chosen to run design systems, which is a population selected for belief in the practice rather than a random cross-section of product organizations. The bottom-up preference is the practitioners' stated value, not a measured outcome comparing the two approaches' adoption rates or defect counts, so this card states it as a documented belief, not as a proven superior method. It is, nonetheless, the strongest evidence this lane has for a question every first-time design-system lead eventually asks out loud: should we design the system first and make products use it, or build products first and extract the system from what works. Evidence class: research evidence, interview-based, small sample, correlational.

This is a question about where a component's content comes from, not about who owns the roadmap once it exists, and the two are separate axes. A centrally run system, GOV.UK's own included, can still source its components bottom-up: GOV.UK's contribution criteria in the Governance section below require a proposer to show a pattern is already **Useful**, evidenced by it being used across live services, before the system adopts it, which is elicitation from existing products, not invention in isolation, even though a dedicated central team makes the final call. A centralized team and a bottom-up sourcing discipline are not in tension; what Lamine and Cheng's participants warned against is a system whose content is invented by a team in isolation and then imposed, regardless of whether that team is centralized, federated or solitary.

## Governance

**Team models.** Curtis names three recurring models for who owns a design system, and their costs travel with them. A solitary model has one team, usually the one that built the first version for its own product, sharing its library outward; Curtis calls this the overlord pattern and the name is the warning: it does not scale past the originating team's own priorities, because nobody else has a seat at the table when the roadmap is set. A centralized model puts a dedicated team in charge, serving every consuming team as a deliberate product; this is the model most public systems in this card's sources run, and its cost is the dedicated team's own headcount and the latency of routing every decision through one group. A federated model distributes ownership across representatives from each consuming product team who decide together; this scales contribution better than the other two but trades it for slower, more negotiated decisions and a real risk of the system drifting toward whichever team shows up most. In practice, most systems this card's sources describe run some hybrid of the three rather than a pure form, evidence class: practitioner heuristic, this card's own synthesis across the sources rather than a single named source's claim.

**Contribution, sized, and mapped to versioning.** Curtis's EightShapes series draws a line this card treats as load-bearing: contribution is not participation. Offering verbal feedback, flagging an issue, or joining a discussion is participation; completing an annotated component design marked ready to code, merging a code fix, or publishing a guideline is a contribution, a measurable, tangible change that moves the system forward. Curtis then sizes contributions on a scale that deliberately echoes semantic versioning's own vocabulary: a **fix** repairs a defect without changing intended behavior (a patch); a **small or large enhancement** extends an existing component or feature, such as adding a new status color to an existing alert, or adding dismissibility to one (a minor change); and a **new feature** is self-evidently new, an entirely new component (a major addition, though not necessarily a breaking one). Curtis is explicit that contributors rarely distinguish a small from a large enhancement cleanly on their own; that is expected, and the conversation that scopes the work is where the distinction actually gets made, not a form field a contributor fills in alone.

**Deprecation: an audit, then a warning-only phase, before removal.** The pattern this card recommends, evidenced by two independently maintained open-source frameworks rather than asserted as abstract best practice, runs: measure who is actually using the thing before touching it, ship a release that changes nothing except adding a visible warning, give consumers a real window to react, then remove it. React 18.3.0 is the cleanest specimen: its release notes describe it as identical to React 18.2 except for warnings about APIs that needed to change ahead of React 19, and the React 19 release notes in turn tell anyone upgrading to go through 18.3.1 first specifically so those warnings surface before anything breaks. Svelte 5's migration guide runs the same pattern with more scaffolding: every breaking change is shown as a before-and-after pair with a "why we did this" callout, old syntax keeps working and can be mixed with new, a migration script automates what it safely can, and a section titled "Things that are not automigrated" honestly lists what a team must still do by hand; Svelte additionally quarantines deprecated features into their own documentation section, labeled as supported for now and eventually removed, so a team on the old path is not stranded without a reference. Evidence class: platform convention, drawn from reading both frameworks' own shipped documentation and changelog at the commits named in the Reading section, not a formal survey of deprecation practice industry-wide.

**GOV.UK's Trial and Stable statuses.** GOV.UK runs two public component statuses. Trial status marks a component or variant that is new, has changed significantly, or needs more real-world testing before the team is confident recommending it everywhere; trial components still have to be usable and accessible in common cases, but may change substantially or even be deprecated once the trial period ends. Six months in trial status is the review point, not an automatic promotion: the design system team weighs the feedback and usage evidence that has come in and decides whether to promote the component to stable, leave it in trial, revise it, or remove it, so the absence of a complaint is evidence the team weighs, not a trigger that promotes the component on its own. GOV.UK pairs this with published contribution criteria that gate two separate moments. To propose a component or pattern, a contributor must show it is **Useful**, evidenced by screenshots or links to the same thing already being used across different services, and **Unique**, meaning it does not duplicate something already in the system, or carries evidence it is better than what it would replace. To move from proposal to publication, the team must show the implementation is **Usable**, tested in user research with a representative sample including disabled users; **Consistent**, reusing existing styles and following both the GOV.UK style guide and the GOV.UK Frontend coding standard; and **Versatile**, flexible enough to serve a range of services and tested across browsers, assistive technologies and devices. Adapted under licence from GOV.UK Design System contribution criteria and component lifecycle statuses, Government Digital Service, Open Government Licence v3.0; see the attribution statement at the foot of this card's Reading section.

**Frost's triage of what an issue actually is.** Brad Frost's governance writing makes a point this card treats as its own short label, a snowflake triage, because his own argument is that no flowchart replaces the judgment call a real conversation requires for each case. He distinguishes four things a consuming team might report as "the design system isn't working": a **bug**, a defect in an existing component, which the system team should patch with urgency because an untrustworthy system loses every team's confidence fast; a **visual discrepancy**, where a design does not match the coded component, which might itself be a system-side bug (design and code libraries out of sync) or a deliberate product-side deviation; a **design system feature**, a new component or variant that plausibly belongs in the shared system but does not exist yet; and a **pattern or recipe**, a composition of existing system components that a product team owns and assembles itself rather than something the system team needs to build. The triage step that actually resolves a discrepancy is a direct question: was this deviation deliberate and justified, for example an A/B test or a time-boxed campaign, in which case the product team carries on and the system team notes a follow-up, or was it drift with no reason behind it, in which case the product realigns to the system. When a feature genuinely belongs in the system but the system team cannot build it inside the product team's deadline, the product team owns the interim work and the system team backlogs the permanent version, rather than either side pretending the deadline does not exist. Frost's closing argument is the one worth carrying forward on its own: a design system team that hears nothing from its consumers should treat silence as a warning sign, not as evidence everything is fine.

## Maturity

Two independently maintained ladders describe how a design system grows, and they describe different things. USWDS's own maturity model, public domain and CC0, states three levels a team can adopt incrementally: **Principles** (the design principles themselves, used as an evaluative lens even before any shared code exists), then **Guidance** (documented patterns and standards consumers can follow without a component library), then **Code** (a shared, reusable implementation). A team does not have to reach Code to get real value out of the earlier levels, and USWDS's own framing treats the ladder as something to climb at whatever pace a team's resources allow, not a race to the top.

Ben Callahan's Sparkbox maturity model, paraphrased with attribution, names four stages a system itself passes through over its life: **building version one**, where the system does not yet exist and the work is getting a first, usable release out the door; **growing adoption**, where the system exists and the challenge shifts to getting product teams to actually use it; **surviving the teenage years**, the stage Callahan singles out as where most systems die, once initial enthusiasm fades and the system needs sustained investment nobody budgeted for past the launch; and **evolving a healthy product**, where the system runs with its own roadmap, backlog and resourcing like any other product the organization ships. Callahan's sharpest warning sits in the second stage: mandated adoption, announced before the system has demonstrated enough value to earn voluntary use, crushes contribution and breeds resentment, because a team forced onto a system that is not yet good enough for their use case experiences the mandate as a tax, not a gift. Evidence class: practitioner heuristic, a consultancy's stage model built from client engagements, not a measured or peer-reviewed study.

## Adoption metrics and how each distorts

Every adoption number this card's sources describe lies in a specific, predictable way, and naming the lie in advance is the only defense a PM has against a number being used to argue something it cannot support.

- **Coverage** (the share of shipped screens, or DOM nodes, that come from system components). Mews built its own coverage metric directly from production DOM data because the simpler proxies did not hold up; coverage distorts through visual-area bias (a large custom hero image section can swamp a screen's percentage even when every interactive control on it is a system component), through unequal component complexity (one hand-rolled data table counts the same as one system-compliant icon in a naive count), and through dynamic pages whose DOM changes shape depending on what data loaded. Productboard independently reached the same conclusion and demoted visual screen coverage to a secondary metric for exactly these reasons.
- **Usage count** (component instance counts, tracked over time). Productboard made this its primary metric, alongside deprecated-component instance counts and React prop usage tracked through code scanning to watch migration progress. A usage count is gameable by leaning on trivial, high-frequency components (a single design system icon used five hundred times inflates the number without a single complex pattern ever being adopted) and says nothing about whether the instances that matter, the ones product and engineering actually argued about, are the ones that moved.
- **Deviations** (custom overrides or non-system instances, counted against system instances). A rising deviation count looks unambiguously bad until Frost's triage above is applied: some deviations are deliberate and justified, an A/B test or a time-boxed campaign, and a metric that cannot tell the difference between sanctioned experimentation and unmanaged drift will either punish legitimate experimentation or hide real drift behind a few well-known exceptions.
- **Deprecated-component instances.** A shrinking count of deprecated components still in use looks like migration progress and can instead mean the tracking itself has been abandoned, components get removed from the audit rather than from the codebase, or the hardest, highest-value instances are the ones left stuck at the tail while the easy ones get counted as wins.

The discipline that actually protects against all four distortions is the same one: before publishing any adoption number, name the decision that changes if the number moves. A coverage figure that triggers no action if it rises or falls is not a metric, it is decoration with a percentage sign on it.

## Tokens

The Design Tokens Community Group's Format Module, version 2025.10, is the closest thing this space has to a shared specification, and it is worth being precise about what it actually is: a first stable Community Group report under the W3C Community Final Specification Agreement (FSA), not a W3C Standard and not on the W3C's Standards Track. A token in this format is a JSON object carrying a required `$value`, an optional `$type` that can be inherited from its enclosing group rather than restated on every token, an optional `$description`, an optional `$deprecated` flag, and an optional `$extensions` object for tool-specific data the spec itself does not define. Tokens nest inside groups, which may declare `$root` and `$extends` behavior, and one token can alias another through a curly-brace reference or a JSON Pointer rather than repeating a value. Composite types, a shadow or a typography style built from several sub-values at once, are part of the same format rather than a separate extension. A companion Resolver Module exists only as a live preview draft that explicitly tells implementers not to build against it yet; this card cites its concepts, sets, modifiers, contexts, only by description, never as something to implement.

Tooling support trails the specification, and a PM should ask exactly how far it trails before assuming "supports design tokens" means the same thing in two different tools. Style Dictionary has had first-class DTCG support since its version 4 ($value, $type, $description, plus a converter from its older v3 format), but its own documentation states plainly that full support for the 2025.10 format is still a work in progress in version 5. Figma's variables feature implements modes, light and dark, or any other named context, as columns inside a variable collection, with the left-most column acting as the default; Figma's own help center documents a native import and export path that maps to DTCG 2025.10 JSON, carrying proprietary extensions (`com.figma.aliasData` for cross-collection aliases, `com.figma.type`) the open format itself does not define. Modes are also a paid feature: only the Education, Professional, Organization and Enterprise plans can create and use them at all, and the number of modes a single collection may hold depends on which of those plans the team is on. This is Figma's own documentation, fetched 2026-09-10, and vendor documentation changes on its own schedule; verify the plan gate before a design sheet promises a client's free-tier team a dark-mode toggle.

The three-tier model this card recommends, **primitive** (raw values with no semantic meaning, the literal hex or pixel number), **semantic** (a name that carries intent and swaps per theme, "color-background-danger" rather than "red-500"), and **component** (a value scoped to one component's own variant), is not this card's invention; it recurs across every token implementation this repository studied closely enough to see the internals. Penpot's own design system implements exactly this split as primary Sass variables, semantic CSS custom properties at global scope that get swapped per theme, and component-scoped CSS custom properties that build variants. Chakra UI separates raw tokens from semantic tokens explicitly, with a semantic token referencing a raw one through a `{colors.red}`-style path and taking conditional values per color mode; daisyUI pairs every semantic surface color with a matching `-content` foreground token so text-on-background contrast is reviewable as a pair rather than a single color in isolation. Nathan Curtis's separate Naming Tokens piece supplies the naming-hygiene layer this structure still needs: a base level built from category, concept and property; modifiers for variant, state, scale and mode; and a namespace for system, theme or domain, so that two tokens with the same short name in two different contexts do not silently collide.

## The design-to-code contract

Treat a token's name as a public API, because every repository this card examined behaves as though it already is one, whether or not anyone wrote that rule down. The clearest illustration comes from Penpot's own internal data model: a shape stores the token it uses by name, not by a stable internal id, so renaming a token, or a whole token group, requires updating every shape that referenced the old name or the reference silently breaks. Penpot changed its copy-paste behavior for exactly this reason, preserving the token reference across a copy instead of resolving it down to a raw value, because resolving it would have quietly severed the shape from future updates to that token. The practical rule this supports: a token rename is a breaking change, and it needs the same discipline a breaking API change gets anywhere else, a deprecation window during which both names resolve, and a named owner who is accountable for the migration actually finishing rather than stalling half-renamed.

**What a round trip loses.** "Supports design tokens" is not one claim, because the exchange format and what survives going design-to-code-to-design is where the actual behavior lives. Penpot exports tokens in the DTCG JSON shape and imports the same, but its own documentation states that export leaves the file's internal, hidden theme out of the exported `$themes` block, and that importing from multiple sources rejects any conflicting token paths outright and silently drops any token type the importing tool does not recognize. A PM accepting a design-to-code handoff should ask which specific token types, and which specific structures (aliases, composite types, theme sets), the tool on each side of the handoff actually reads and writes, rather than taking a vendor's "DTCG-compatible" claim as a guarantee nothing is lost.

**Theme and set model.** A theme, in the token sense this card uses throughout, is a named bundle of values for the semantic tier, swapped as a unit; Penpot's own internal model keeps token sets, themes, and which themes are currently active as three separate concepts precisely because a theme switch should only ever touch semantic-tier values, never reach down and rewrite a component-tier override that a product team deliberately set.

**Source of truth and sync direction.** Name, explicitly and in writing, whether design or code is authoritative for a given token or component, and who is responsible for keeping the other side in sync, design to code, code to design, or genuinely both with a reconciliation process, because every maintained component library this card studied treats this as an open, sometimes unsolved, problem rather than a solved one. MUI's own Figma Sync plugin, which kept a Figma library in step with the coded theme, was suspended in 2024; most of shadcn/ui's available Figma kits are unofficial, third-party and community-maintained, and shadcn's own documentation disclaims them rather than vouching for their fidelity; Chakra UI is the exception among the libraries studied here with an official kit it maintains itself. A design-to-code contract that does not name which side wins when the two disagree, and how a disagreement even gets detected, is not yet a contract.

**Plugin and design-agent permission scopes, as a vendor-risk question, not a productivity one.** Penpot's plugin manifest model declares scoped permissions, content, library, user and comment read and write, download access, clipboard access, where a write permission implies the matching read, and any fetch call a plugin makes strips outgoing credentials before it leaves the sandbox. Penpot states outright that it sets no requirements on plugin security or quality; a plugin is hosted and maintained by whoever wrote it. The same question sharpens further once an AI agent, not a person, holds the keys: Penpot's own MCP server can let an agent read and write the page currently in focus, including executing arbitrary Plugin API JavaScript through an execute-code call, and Penpot's own guidance for that capability is to start read-only, have the agent describe a change before applying it, and keep every step small and reversible; recent releases hardened this further by restricting the developer REPL endpoint to development environments only and disabling developer tools in multi-user mode. Treat a design tool's plugin ecosystem and its AI write-access the way any other system's third-party integration and agent permissions get treated: a scoped review at acceptance, not a checkbox ticked because a feature sounded convenient in a demo. Paraphrased with attribution from Penpot's own documentation (`penpot/penpot`, commit `37dab75`), MPL-2.0; see the Reading section for the reuse class this repository applies to it.

## Ownership models

Five actively maintained, MIT-licensed component libraries, paraphrased with attribution, sort into three distinct ownership models for how a product actually takes code from a shared system, and each answers "who pulls upstream fixes" differently.

**Copy the code in.** shadcn/ui describes itself, pointedly, as not a component library but a way to build one: its CLI copies each component's source directly into the product's own codebase, where it becomes the product team's own code from that moment on. Only the headless primitive underneath, Radix, Base UI or React Aria, shadcn publishes the same component three times, once per primitive, marked by a `base` field in its docs, stays an installed dependency that keeps receiving the primitive maintainer's own upstream fixes. After the copy, every visual bug, every accessibility regression and every future upgrade belongs to the product team, not to shadcn, because there is no longer a shadcn version of that file to pull a fix from. A PM accepting components built this way should name, in writing, who on the team now owns that copied file, because "the library handles it" stops being true the moment the copy happens.

**Install a dependency.** MUI and Chakra both ship as an installed package a product imports and upgrades on the vendor's own release schedule, which means the product also inherits the vendor's own known gaps wholesale rather than selectively. MUI's own per-component accessibility documentation is the most concrete evidence this card has for taking that inheritance seriously: it publishes a WCAG 2.2 conformance report per component, in VPAT-style language, scoring each success criterion on two independent axes, whether the component conforms (supports, partially supports, does not support, not applicable) and who is responsible for that criterion (the component itself, a shared layer, or the author using it), with a separate flag for ratings assessed from source code but not yet proven by an actual test. Those same reports record real, specific gaps in MUI's own defaults: its info and warning button colors fall below 4.5:1 contrast in every variant the library ships, a loading button state adds no live region so a screen-reader user may never learn the action is in progress, and only 12 of the library's 63 component folders have a report published at all. Installing a dependency means accepting that gap list as the product's own gap list at acceptance, carrying each entry into the risk register, not assuming a well-known library's reputation substitutes for reading what its own documentation admits.

**CSS-only classes.** daisyUI ships no JavaScript behavior at all, only class names applied to native HTML, which means the entire keyboard-interaction and ARIA-state burden sits with whoever writes the markup in the product; daisyUI's own per-component accessibility documentation, where a page for it exists, reads "soon." Semantic UI, now effectively dormant (last release 2.5.0, October 2022, read here as historical reference rather than current practice), ran the same CSS-only model but left one governance idea worth keeping regardless of the project's own fate: it layered a default theme, a packaged theme and a site theme, each with its own separate `.variables` and `.overrides` file, so that any visual deviation from the default had one specific, documented file it lived in rather than an untracked override buried somewhere in application code. That override-layer discipline is a usable model for a design-debt register independent of whether anyone still runs Semantic UI itself.

## The service-standard layer

Above any single component, pattern, or even the whole visual system sits a layer that asks about the service as a whole, and a component can pass every check this card describes and the service can still fail, because nothing at the component level asks whether the right problem got solved end to end. GOV.UK's Service Standard states this as fourteen points, adapted under licence with attribution: understand users and their needs; solve a whole problem for users; provide a joined-up experience across all channels; make the service simple to use; make sure everyone can use the service; have a multidisciplinary team; use agile ways of working; iterate and improve frequently; create a secure service which protects users' privacy; define what success looks like and publish performance data; choose the right tools and technology; make new source code open; use and contribute to open standards, common components and patterns; and operate a reliable service. USWDS states the same altitude as five design principles rather than fourteen service points, tied explicitly to the 21st Century Integrated Digital Experience Act: start with real user needs; earn trust (through transparency, reliability and stewardship of the data a service collects); embrace accessibility; promote continuity (consistency across a whole agency's services, not just one); and listen (building feedback and iteration into the service's own operation, not treating launch as the finish line).

Both lists share the same argument this card treats as the point worth carrying forward: a design system makes consistency cheap at the component level, and neither GOV.UK's fourteen points nor USWDS's five principles are answerable by any component, however well it conforms. "Solve a whole problem for users" and "understand context" are organizational and service-design questions, not screen-design ones, which is exactly why this layer sits above the design system rather than inside it, and why a team that has adopted a design system perfectly can still ship a service that fails its users.

## The trap: adherence as a quality proxy

A component-by-component conformance score is seductively easy to produce and easy to mistake for proof the product actually works. Treating strict adherence to a design system as the measure of quality substitutes a process metric for an outcome one, in exactly the shape [Usability Heuristics](usability-heuristics.md) warns against with a violation count: a screen built entirely from system components, with zero deviations flagged, can still fail the task its user actually came to complete, because the system has nothing to say about whether the right components were assembled into the right flow. Mandating adherence compounds the trap rather than fixing it. Ben Callahan's maturity model names this directly: a mandate announced before the system has earned voluntary use, through a proven ability to solve real product problems, crushes contribution and breeds the exact resentment that makes the next release harder to land, not easier. Karen VanHouten's review of recurring design-system failures, drawn from her own practice across 2006, 2016 and 2023 and from responses she solicited publicly, names five patterns that recur regardless of the tooling era: no shared, contextual sense of purpose (a system whose stated goal is "improve the UX of our products," which rules nothing out and therefore decides nothing); overbuilding or scaling too early, building for an organization-wide rollout before anyone validated the approach at small scale; an inability to make decisions and move forward quickly; a lack of clear ownership and dedicated resources, a part-time team asked to do a full-time job; and a lack of cultural alignment, where the organization's incentives quietly reward teams for building their own thing rather than contributing to the shared one. VanHouten frames all five as cultural failures, not component-level ones, which is the same claim this card's trap section makes from a different angle: you cannot inspect your way out of a governance problem by adding more rules to the component library. Itai Vonshak's 2025 piece for UX Magazine, read here strictly as one practitioner's opinion rather than as evidence, argues the opposite failure mode from the same root cause: design systems that became rigid rulebooks and unread documentation, a maintenance burden that fails both small teams and large multi-product ones, and adherence that guarantees nothing about whether the resulting product is actually good. Cite Vonshak as what he is, one named practitioner's argument, written partly to build the case for the AI-assisted tooling he is developing, not as measured evidence that rigidity is widespread.

## How it lies: the ROI ladder

Every public figure claiming a design system's return on investment in this card's sources is either a small, self-timed vendor study or an openly hypothetical model, and no peer-reviewed study of design-system ROI or adoption turned up in this repository's search of academic sources. Rank the figures in this card's sources by how much weight their own methodology can actually bear, because they get quoted in the opposite order surprisingly often:

- **Sparkbox, 47 percent faster.** A vendor study (n = 8 developers) had participants code a simple contact form, once from scratch and once using IBM's Carbon Design System; the Carbon builds came in at a median of roughly two hours against 4.2 hours from scratch, including the time spent learning Carbon itself, and were independently rated higher for visual consistency. Evidence class: vendor claim, single firm, no randomization, a sample of eight.
- **Figma, 34 percent faster.** A vendor-run, counterbalanced experiment had designers complete a bank-app design task, once with an up-to-date, task-relevant design system and once without; Figma's own write-up is explicit that it frames 34 percent as close to the maximum realistic saving a team should expect, not a typical or guaranteed one. Evidence class: vendor claim, the vendor's own stated ceiling.
- **Smashing Magazine, 135 percent, plus or minus 25 percent.** A fully worked formula, built on Ben Callahan's design-system efficiency curve, applied to an invented company the article calls Acme, Inc., with assumptions for a five-year system life, a six-to-twelve-month ramp-up, and parameters for team share and efficiency gain the reader is meant to plug their own numbers into. The article's own conclusion rounds its worked example to "we estimate 135 percent ROI... (plus or minus 25 percent)" as a way of making a fully hypothetical calculation look like a measured result. Evidence class: vendor claim, explicitly hypothetical, no product's real data behind the headline number.
- **zeroheight's annual Design Systems Report.** A vendor survey (2026 edition, 147 self-selected respondents, roughly 90 percent from North America, Western Europe and Oceania) reports figures like buy-in satisfaction sitting between 42 and 32 percent and adoption as the top-reported challenge for a fifth consecutive year; any specific number from this report needs its sample size and the self-selected, vendor-run nature of the survey quoted alongside it, never stated as an industry-wide fact on its own.

Never collapse two of these figures from different sources, different methods and different years into one combined claim, "91 percent trust against 38 percent adoption" is exactly the kind of sentence this warning exists to prevent: two numbers that were never measured together, from two different surveys with two different samples, stitched into a single statistic that sounds more precise than anything either source actually supports. A design-system ROI claim earns a citation naming its source, its sample, and its evidence class every single time it appears in a document, never a bare percentage.
