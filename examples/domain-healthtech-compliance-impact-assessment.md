## 1. Product and data summary

- What the feature does and who uses it: Cobaltine takes a user-entered symptom plus three or four self-reported attributes, and returns a ranked list of possible causes with a triage suggestion (self-care, see a doctor within a week, or seek emergency care), for informational use. The user is the consumer who downloaded the app, not a clinician, and not a payer; there is no clinician seat and no integration into an EHR.
- Markets and jurisdictions in scope: United States federal (FDA device and software-function framing, FTC), US state-level on privacy (Washington, and the states tracked in the state-privacy patchwork at launch), and a planned European Union launch in the following quarter (GDPR). The sections below answer per market where the answer differs.
- Does the product contain an AI model making or influencing decisions about people? Yes. The symptom-to-ranked-list step is an AI model, and the triage suggestion it feeds out influences what a consumer does next (self-care, book an appointment, or call emergency services). The [AI overlay](../templates/ai/human-approval-gates.md) fires: there is no human approval gate before the triage line reaches the consumer, so that gate's design (what "human approval" means on a self-serve app, and whether one can exist at all at this volume) is an open item carried in section 8.

**Intended-use sentence, written the way a regulator reads it:** "Cobaltine surfaces a ranked list of possible causes for a user-entered symptom and a triage suggestion (self-care, see a doctor within a week, seek emergency care), for informational use; it does not diagnose and does not replace a clinician's evaluation." That sentence is the sorting fact the rest of this document follows from, not a summary of the rest. The domain card at [knowledge/domains/healthtech.md](../knowledge/domains/healthtech.md) states the same rule plainly: intended use, the claim about what the software does, decides whether the software is an app or a device, and the class it lands in after that decides the evidence burden, not the other way around.

**The honest intended-use self-assessment, section 2 continued.** This is where the product earns or loses its answer, so it is worked against the criteria rather than asserted. The four CDS criteria (21st Century Cures section 3060, as interpreted in FDA's CDS guidance) all must be met for a software function to sit inside the non-device CDS carve-out. Cobaltine is checked against each, and one failure takes the whole function out of the carve-out:

1. Not intended to acquire, analyze, or process medical information where the clinician or patient does not review the results. Cobaltine's consumer DOES review the results (they see the list and the triage line before acting), so this criterion reads met, on its own, but with no clinician present the criterion's logic was written for a clinician-facing decision support tool; counsel (Daniel Cho) flags this as the weakest met criterion because there is no clinician to be "substantially influencing" at all.
2. Not intended to provide a patient-specific diagnosis or treatment recommendation to a clinician or patient that is intended to replace or substantially influence a clinician's independent judgment. This is the load-bearing criterion and it fails. Cobaltine is sold direct to consumers with no clinician in the loop, so there is no clinician whose independent judgment the software could "substantially influence": the consumer is the one receiving what the app frames as a "recommendation." Counsel treats the criterion as not met precisely because there is no clinician present, not in Cobaltine's favor. This is the criterion that pulls the triage line toward device software, and it cannot be waved through with "we are informational."
3. Not intended to acquire, analyze, or process medical images or signals. Cobaltine processes text, not imaging or waveforms. Reads met, and this is genuinely a clean yes rather than a self-serving one.
4. Not intended to display, analyze, or print medical images or signals for medical purposes. Same reading: met.

So the honest score is two met cleanly (3 and 4), one met with a flagged weakness because there is no clinician in the loop for the criterion's premise to even exist (1), and one NOT met (2). All four are required for the carve-out; therefore Cobaltine's self-assessment does not claim the CDS carve-out on the consumer-facing triage function as currently worded and positioned, and holds classification open (section 8).

**IMDRF drive reading, against the same output.** IMDRF grades SaMD on the state of the healthcare situation and how much the software drives the clinical management. The three bands the domain card names are *inform*, *drive*, and *diagnose or treat*. Cobaltine's self-assessment places the consumer-facing triage suggestion at **inform clinical management** (the lowest severity-and-drive band the consumer can land in), on this logic: the condition could be urgent (the "seek emergency care" line is triggered by alarming features, so the situation is not low-risk), but the software does not itself take the treatment action, the consumer does. Counsel's honest flag on that reading is that an "inform" placement rests on the software's *not* being the actor, while its severity band (life-threatening, because the emergency line exists) is the band where an inform placement most easily flips to a drive placement if the UI wording drifts toward a command ("go now") instead of a suggestion ("consider seeking"). That drift is a copy-and-clinical-review control, recorded in section 8.

**HIPAA dual-channel test, section 2 continued.** The scoping fact is who holds the covered-entity relationship, and Cobaltine has not fixed it yet.

- Sold **direct to consumers** (current primary): Cobaltine is not a HIPAA covered entity and not a business associate on its own; HIPAA's Privacy Rule and Security Rule do not reach it on their own. The operative privacy surface becomes state privacy law (WA MHMD, and the patchwork) plus the FTC surface (the HBNR, section 5 of the Act). This is the channel where "we are not covered" is the true answer, and where the HBNR is the gap-filler that still binds.
- Sold **through a health system** (planned): if that channel means Cobaltine processes PHI for the health system (its covered-entity status), Cobaltine is a business associate for that data, and HIPAA's obligations plus a signed BAA attach before the first byte, per [healthtech.md](../knowledge/domains/healthtech.md)'s question 4 (PHI flow, BAA before the first byte). The de-identified wellness version of the product (no PHI crossing the boundary, per the card's section-2 PHI-row pattern) is the structure that keeps Cobaltine OUT of BA status on that channel; the PHI-sharing version is the structure that puts it in. ## 4. DPIA flag

Honest answers, then the requirement:

- Systematic profiling or automated decisions with significant effects? Yes, on a bounded reading. Cobaltine does not make a decision about the person in a legal sense, but it does make an automated output that can carry a significant effect on a consumer (the "seek emergency care" line can drive them to call emergency services, and an "informational" output could, wrongly, drive them to self-treat an urgent condition). [healthtech.md](../knowledge/domains/healthtech.md)'s adverse-event question (Q7) is the same fact framed operationally.
- Large-scale processing of special category data? Yes. A consumer symptom app at scale processes health data (GDPR Art. 9) for a large number of data subjects; that is exactly the trigger.
- Systematic monitoring of a publicly accessible area? No. There is no physical-space monitoring; this is a self-serve input, not surveillance.
- New technology applied to personal data in a novel way? Yes. AI symptom-to-ranked-cause-to-triage on free text, presented to a lay consumer without a clinician gate, is the novel application that makes the output's error consequences asymmetric (a false "self-care" on an urgent symptom is the asymmetric harm).

**DPIA required:** yes · If yes: run by Priya Venkatesan, DPO, due 2026-12-15, filed at the data-protection register. (Under GDPR the DPIA is mandatory on the large-scale special-category test alone; under WA MHMD the equivalent is the data-minimization and security review the statute requires, which the same runner owns. This is its own exercise; this section only decides that it is required and who runs it.)

## 7. Retention and deletion

- Retention schedule per category is complete in section 3: confirmed, with two fields marked [OPEN] where the vendor's own retention and the EU architecture are still being confirmed; an open field is an open field, not a gap quietly closed.
- Deletion is implemented, not just promised: a deletion request executes through the app's request flow (WA MHMD's 45-day response period, extendable once by a further 45 days when reasonably necessary, with deletion from archived or backup systems permitted up to six months after the request is authenticated), which issues a deletion command against the live backend tables and against the derived triage-result records; Bastien Morel owns the mechanism, Priya Venkatesan owns the SLA against the clock. It is a command that runs against production stores, not a checkbox in a policy document.
- What survives deletion (backups, logs, aggregates) and for how long: nightly encrypted backups retained 30 days (so a deleted record reappears from a backup only inside that 30-day window, then ages out); session and security logs retained 90 days independently of the deletion command, and counsel (Daniel Cho) flags that those 90-day logs are the residual risk a user is told is "deleted" while it still exists; aggregate safety-analytics tables are retained indefinitely in de-identified or pseudonymized form for adverse-event and near-miss review, and counsel flags these are NOT within a consumer's MHMD deletion right only if they are genuinely non-re-identifiable, which is a test, not an assumption.
