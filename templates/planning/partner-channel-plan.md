## 3. Partner economics: their margin and our net revenue

<!-- Write the partner's side first. A margin that does not pay for the partner's
     effort buys a signed agreement and no deals. Some resellers earn more from
     the services they attach to a product than from the resale margin itself;
     ask them, and if that is true here, it is their real incentive, so write it in.

     The arithmetic, per deal, for each type in scope:
     - Reseller: our net revenue = list price × (1 minus partner discount).
       Partner margin = the partner's sale price minus our net revenue, plus any
       services the partner bills on the deal.
     - Referral: referral fee = fee rate × contract value for the stated term.
       Our net revenue = contract value minus the fee.
     - Marketplace: our net revenue = transaction value × (1 minus marketplace fee
       rate). Where a reseller sits inside a marketplace deal, both deductions
       apply, and the marketplace's current terms say which price each is taken from.
     - Co-sell: usually no margin; the partner's incentive is something else, such
       as quota credit or consumption on their platform. Name it.
     - Direct, for comparison: direct net = list price × (1 minus average direct
       discount) minus direct cost of sale per deal.
     - Every type: our net after partner cost = our net revenue minus what the
       partner costs us per deal (partner manager time, enablement, development
       funds) minus our own cost of sale on the deal, where our sellers still do
       the selling (referral, co-sell, a marketplace private offer).

     A channel whose net after partner cost is below direct net earns its place
     in one of two ways: those deals would not happen direct at all, or the
     channel wins so much more often that it is worth more per opportunity
     worked (compare net after partner cost × win rate for the channel against
     the same product for direct). Say which, and cite the evidence from section 1.

     Do not set the price a reseller charges its customer. Dictating resale prices
     is restricted by competition law in many jurisdictions; any rule touching a
     partner's price goes to counsel before it goes in this file. -->

## 9. How this plan fails

| Failure | The tell | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Partner count as the goal | Signed partners rises every quarter while active partners stays flat | Report active against signed, and tier on activity, never on signature |
| Influenced pipeline added to sourced | The partner slide claims a share of pipeline that, added to direct, exceeds the whole | Separate columns, the definitions in section 8, and one owner who classifies disputed deals |
| A margin that does not pay for the effort | Registrations arrive and age out with no recorded activity | Rebuild section 3 from the partner's own profit and loss, services included, and ask two partners whether it is right |
| Direct sellers fighting partners | Registrations approved slowly or rejected, and accounts quietly re-entered as direct | Compensation neutrality written in section 6, an approval time someone is held to, and an escalation owner outside sales |
| Two prices for one customer | A buyer holds a direct quote and a partner quote for the same deal | The registration rule binds our direct team's quoting; never fix the partner's own resale price |
| Enablement measured by attendance | Partners are certified, and every first call still needs our sales engineer | Certification becomes a test the partner's seller passes alone, and section 3's partner cost is re-priced |
| A listing mistaken for a channel | The marketplace listing is live and has produced no pipeline | Treat a listing as a procurement path for buyers already in motion, and pair it with co-sell or our own demand |
